§15.01 CRIMINAL ATTEMPT ▾

§15.01 – CRIMINAL ATTEMPT

You don't have to actually succeed at a crime to be charged — taking a meaningful step toward committing one is enough.

What It Is

A person commits Criminal Attempt if, with specific intent to commit a particular offense, they take an act that goes beyond mere preparation — meaning they've crossed from planning into doing — but the offense is never completed.

Key distinction: Buying a gun to commit murder = preparation (not yet an attempt). Driving to the victim's house with the loaded gun = attempt. The line is crossed when conduct directly tends toward committing the offense, even if the final step never happens.

Special Rules

  • Aggravated versions: If the target offense could be aggravated (e.g., simple assault → aggravated assault), and an aggravating element is present during the attempt, the charge is attempt of the aggravated offense — not just the base offense.
  • No defense that the crime was completed: If a suspect attempts an offense and it actually succeeds, that doesn't get them out of the attempt charge (though they'd typically be charged with the completed offense instead).

Classification — Sliding Scale

There is no single fixed classification for Criminal Attempt. The grade is always one step below the offense attempted:

Attempted OffenseAttempt Classification
Capital FelonyFelony of the 1st Degree
Felony of the 1st DegreeFelony of the 2nd Degree
Felony of the 2nd DegreeFelony of the 3rd Degree
Felony of the 3rd DegreeState Jail Felony
State Jail FelonyClass A Misdemeanor (explicit statutory floor)
Class A MisdemeanorClass B Misdemeanor
Class B MisdemeanorClass C Misdemeanor
Special exception (§15.01(e)): Attempting to commit Capital Murder of a peace officer under §19.03(a)(1) is a Felony of the 1st Degree punishable by 25–99 years or life in TDCJ — not reducible below F1 regardless of the normal sliding scale.
Note on Class C attempts: The statute does not address attempting a Class C misdemeanor — there is no recognized grade below Class C.
§15.02 CRIMINAL CONSPIRACY ▾

§15.02 – CRIMINAL CONSPIRACY

Agreeing with someone to commit a felony — and taking any step toward it — is itself a crime, even if the felony never happens.

What It Is

Criminal Conspiracy requires two elements, both present:

  • Agreement: The suspect agrees with one or more other people that at least one of them will engage in conduct constituting a felony.
  • Overt act: The suspect or at least one co-conspirator performs any act — no matter how small — in furtherance of the plan.
The agreement can be inferred from conduct. No written agreement or explicit verbal pact is required. If everyone's behavior shows they're working toward a common criminal goal, a jury can find a conspiracy.

Felonies Only

Criminal Conspiracy under §15.02 only applies to felony target offenses. You cannot be charged with conspiracy to commit a misdemeanor under this section.

Things That Are NOT a Defense

  • One co-conspirator isn't criminally responsible (e.g., is legally insane)
  • A co-conspirator was acquitted — unless all co-conspirators except one have been acquitted
  • A co-conspirator hasn't been prosecuted, got a deal, or is immune
  • The suspect belongs to a class of people who legally can't commit the target offense individually (they can still conspire to help others commit it)
  • The target offense was actually committed

Classification — Sliding Scale

The grade is one step below the most serious felony that is the object of the conspiracy:

Target FelonyConspiracy Classification
Capital FelonyFelony of the 1st Degree
Felony of the 1st DegreeFelony of the 2nd Degree
Felony of the 2nd DegreeFelony of the 3rd Degree
Felony of the 3rd DegreeState Jail Felony
State Jail FelonyClass A Misdemeanor
Note: This section does not apply to conspiracy under §76.02 (combination of criminal street gangs with foreign terrorist organizations — that offense has its own provisions).
§15.03 CRIMINAL SOLICITATION ▾

§15.03 – CRIMINAL SOLICITATION

Trying to get someone else to commit a capital felony or first-degree felony — even if they refuse — is itself a serious felony.

What It Is

A person commits Criminal Solicitation if, with intent that a capital felony or first-degree felony be committed, they request, command, or attempt to induce another person to commit that offense (or to become a party to it).

The solicited person doesn't have to agree. The offense is complete the moment the request or command is made with the required intent.

Applies Only to Capital and F1 Offenses

This is a high-bar statute — it only applies when the target offense is a Capital Felony or Felony of the 1st Degree. Soliciting lesser offenses falls outside §15.03 (see §15.031 for solicitation of a minor).

Corroboration Required

A conviction cannot rest on the uncorroborated testimony of the person allegedly solicited alone. The solicitation must also be shown by circumstances strongly corroborating both the solicitation itself and the actor's intent. This is a protection against false accusations.

Things That Are NOT a Defense

  • The person solicited isn't criminally responsible
  • The person solicited was acquitted or not prosecuted
  • The actor belongs to a class that can't commit the target offense individually
  • The target offense was actually committed

Classification

  • Soliciting a Capital Felony → Felony of the 1st Degree
  • Soliciting a Felony of the 1st Degree → Felony of the 2nd Degree
§15.031 CRIMINAL SOLICITATION OF A MINOR ▾

§15.031 – CRIMINAL SOLICITATION OF A MINOR

Trying to get a minor (under 17) to commit serious offenses — particularly sexual offenses — is a standalone crime, regardless of whether the minor complies.

Two Tracks

Track 1 — Serious listed offenses (42A.054): A person solicits a minor to engage in conduct constituting any offense listed under Art. 42A.054(a) of the Code of Criminal Procedure (these are the most serious violent and sexual offenses for which deferred adjudication is prohibited).

Track 2 — Sexual offense solicitation: A person solicits a minor — or someone they believe to be a minor — to engage in conduct constituting a specific list of sexual offenses including: §§20A.02(a)(7)/(8), 21.02, 21.03, 21.11, 22.011, 22.021, 43.02, 43.021, 43.05(a)(2), or 43.25.

Key Definition

"Minor" means an individual younger than 17 years of age. (Note: different from the general definition used elsewhere; 17-year-olds are not minors under this section.)

Corroboration Required

Same as §15.03 — a conviction cannot rest solely on the uncorroborated testimony of the minor allegedly solicited.

Things That Are NOT a Defense

  • The minor isn't criminally responsible for the solicited offense
  • The minor was acquitted or not prosecuted
  • The actor belongs to a class that can't commit the offense individually
  • The solicited offense was actually committed

Classification — Sliding Scale with Enhancement

Base rule: one category lower than the solicited offense.

Gang enhancement — same category as the solicited offense: If it is shown at trial that the actor was 17 or older AND a member of a criminal street gang, AND committed the offense to further gang activities or avoid detection as a gang member, the classification is elevated to the same grade as the solicited offense — the one-step reduction disappears.
§15.032 CHILD GROOMING ▾

§15.032 – CHILD GROOMING

Manipulating, coaxing, or enticing a child under 18 toward sexual activity or sex trafficking — even without touching them — is a felony.

What It Is

A person commits Child Grooming if, with the intent that a sexual offense under Ch. 43 or a sexual activity offense under Ch. 20A, 21, or 22 be committed, they knowingly:

  • Persuade, induce, entice, or coerce — or attempt to do any of these — a child younger than 18 to engage in specific conduct that would constitute one of those sexual offenses (or make the child a party to such an offense).

The offense is complete upon the persuasion/enticement — no actual sexual contact is required.

Classification

  • Base offense: Felony of the 3rd Degree
  • Enhanced to Felony of the 2nd Degree if the actor has a prior conviction for: trafficking (Ch. 20A sexual conduct), Continuous Sexual Abuse (§21.02 or §21.03), Indecency with a Child (§21.11), Sexual Assault of a child under 18 (§22.011), or Aggravated Sexual Assault of a child under 18 (§22.021).

Affirmative Defense

It is an affirmative defense if the actor is under 18 and the conduct occurred between the actor and another child under 18 who is either: (a) in a dating relationship and not more than 3 years older or younger, or (b) the actor's spouse — and the conduct occurred only between those two.

Double Jeopardy Note

If the conduct also constitutes an offense under another code section, the actor may be prosecuted under either section but not both.

§15.04 RENUNCIATION DEFENSE ▾

§15.04 – RENUNCIATION DEFENSE

Voluntarily abandoning a criminal plan — and actively preventing the crime from happening — can be a complete defense to attempt, conspiracy, or solicitation charges.

How It Works

Renunciation is an affirmative defense — the defendant must raise and prove it. It applies differently to each of the three preparatory offenses:

  • Criminal Attempt (§15.01): The defendant, under circumstances showing a voluntary and complete renunciation of the criminal objective, either (a) abandoned the conduct and that was enough to prevent the crime, or (b) took further affirmative action that actually prevented the crime.
  • Criminal Conspiracy (§15.02) or Criminal Solicitation (§15.03): The defendant countermanded (took back) the solicitation or withdrew from the conspiracy before the target offense was committed, and took further affirmative action that actually prevented the target offense.

What Is NOT Voluntary Renunciation

Renunciation is not considered voluntary if the change of heart was caused by:

  • New circumstances that increased the risk of getting caught (e.g., a police car shows up)
  • New circumstances that made the crime harder to complete
  • A decision to simply postpone the crime to another time or switch to a similar target
In plain terms: cold feet driven by fear of getting caught doesn't count. Genuine moral abandonment that actually stops the crime does.

Mitigation at Sentencing

Even if renunciation doesn't qualify as a full defense, evidence that the defendant renounced their criminal objective and made a substantial effort to prevent the offense is admissible at punishment. If a jury finds genuine renunciation at sentencing, the punishment grade is reduced by one level from whatever the offense was found to be.

§15.05 NO OFFENSE ▾

§15.05 – NO OFFENSE

You cannot be charged with "attempting to attempt" or "conspiring to conspire."

What It Means

Attempting, conspiring to commit, or soliciting a preparatory offense defined in this chapter is not a criminal offense. In other words:

  • Attempting a Criminal Attempt → not an offense
  • Conspiring to commit a Criminal Conspiracy → not an offense
  • Soliciting someone to commit Criminal Solicitation → not an offense

This prevents pyramiding of preparatory offenses. The law draws the line at one step removed from the completed crime — it doesn't extend to attempts of attempts or conspiracies of conspiracies.
