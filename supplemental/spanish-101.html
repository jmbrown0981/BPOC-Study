Spanish 101 — How the BPOC Phrases Actually Work

The BPOC teaches Spanish phrase-for-phrase. This page explains why each phrase is built the way it is: how verbs change form, why “usted” takes the “he/she” verb, where the pronouns go in a command, and why ¿Quiere que llame una grúa? means “Do you want me to call a tow truck?”

Where the stress falls

  • Words ending in a vowel, n, or s are stressed on the next-to-last syllable: MA-no, HA-blan.
  • Words ending in any other consonant are stressed on the last syllable: ha-BLAR, es-pa-ÑOL.
  • A written accent overrides both rules: vehículo (veh-EE-cu-lo). That's why commands pick up accents when pronouns are attached (siéntese, golpéalo) — see section 4.
  • Some accents only tell two words apart: sí (yes) vs. si (if); tú (you) vs. tu (your); él (he) vs. el (the); qué? (what?) vs. que (that). The instructor alternatives on the card (“¿ambulancia, sí o no?”) need the accent on sí.
  • The little word a (to, at) never takes an accent. The BPOC handout writes it BPOC: á in several phrases; that's an old typewriter habit, not standard spelling.
2Usted vs. Tú — two words for “you”▾

English has one “you.” Spanish has two for one person: tú (informal — friends, family, children) and usted (formal — strangers, adults, anyone you're showing respect to). On duty, default to usted. It's respectful, and it's what most of the BPOC phrases use.

PersonPronounEnglish
1st singularyoI
2nd singular, informaltúyou (friend, child)
3rd singularél / ella / ustedhe / she / you (formal)
1st pluralnosotros / nosotraswe
3rd pluralellos / ellas / ustedesthey / you all

The key idea: usted uses the “he/she” verb

Usted started out as a title (roughly “your grace”), so grammatically it is treated like a third person. It always takes the same verb form as he/she. That's why one verb form can mean three things:

¿Habla inglés?→ “Does he speak English?” / “Does she speak English?” / “Do you speak English?”
Adding the pronoun removes the doubt: ¿Habla usted inglés? = “Do you speak English?” (BPOC 26.5)

Why you'll see both on the card

Most BPOC commands are in the usted form (siéntese, levántese, no se mueva), but some are tú forms because that's how they're said on the street or to a younger person: suelta el arma, cruza los pies, dame la otra mano, and all of the 26.3 “danger words” (desármalo, pégale), which are what suspects say to each other. Section 9 lists both command forms for every verb so you can recognize either.

Dropping the pronoun

Because the verb ending already shows who is acting, Spanish usually leaves the subject pronoun out: Hablo sólo un poco de español = “(I) speak only a little Spanish.” No entiendo = “(I) don't understand.” Put the pronoun in only for emphasis or to make “you” clear (¿Usted llamó a la policía?).

3How Verbs Work — stem + ending▾

Every Spanish verb has a dictionary form (the infinitive, “to ___”) ending in -ar, -er, or -ir. Take off those two letters to get the stem, then add an ending that shows who is doing it. English mostly uses separate words (I / you / we) to show who; Spanish builds it into the verb.

Present tense — regular endings

Who-ar: trabajar (to work)-er: beber (to drink)-ir: vivir (to live)
yo (I)trabajobebovivo
tú (you, informal)trabajasbebesvives
él / ella / ustedtrabajabebevive
nosotros (we)trabajamosbebemosvivimos
ellos / ellas / ustedestrabajanbebenviven

One Spanish present-tense form covers several English ones: trabajo = “I work,” “I am working,” and “I do work.” Spanish doesn't need “do” for questions or negatives either: ¿Entiende? = “Do you understand?”; No entiendo = “I don't understand.”

Three kinds of “irregular”

  • Stem-changing STEM: the vowel in the stem changes when it's stressed — everywhere except nosotros. entender → entiendo, sentarse → me siento, acostarse → me acuesto, repetir → repito. The three patterns are e→ie, o→ue, and e→i.
  • Spelling changes: some letters change to keep the sound: apagar → apague (keeps the hard g), cruzar → cruce, brincar → brinque. These show up in commands.
  • Truly irregular IRR: the most common verbs (ser, estar, ir, dar, hacer, salir, venir, haber) have to be memorized. They're also the ones you'll hear most.

4Commands — how “sit down,” “get out,” and “don't move” are built▾

Most of 26.5–26.8 is commands. There are two sets, matching the two words for “you.”

Usted commands (use these on duty)

Start from the yo form, drop the -o, and add the “opposite” vowel: -ar verbs take -e, and -er/-ir verbs take -a. The same form works for positive and negative.

Infinitiveyo formUsted commandNegative
firmar (sign)firmofirme aquí — sign hereno firme
mover (move)muevomuevano mueva la cabeza — don't move your head
salir (get out)salgosalga del vehículo — get out of the vehicleno salga
repetir (repeat)repitorepita, por favorno repita
apagar (turn off)apagoapague el motor (gu keeps the hard g)no apague

Because they start from the yo form, irregular yo forms carry through: salgo → salga, hago → haga, vengo → venga, pongo → ponga. Only a few are fully irregular: ir → vaya, ser → sea, dar → dé, estar → esté.

Tú commands (what you'll hear between suspects)

The positive tú command usually looks just like the he/she present form: suelta (drop), cruza (cross), corre (run), dispara (shoot). A few short irregulars: sal (get out), ven (come), pon (put), haz (do), ve (go). The negative tú command is different — it uses the usted form plus -s: no corras, no te muevas.

Where pronouns go in a command

  • Positive command: pronoun attaches to the end, making one word: siéntese (sit yourself), déme (give me), golpéalo (beat him).
  • Negative command: pronoun moves in front, as a separate word: no se mueva (don't move yourself), no me mire (don't look at me).
  • The accent rule: attaching a pronoun adds a syllable, so the stress would land in the wrong place. A written accent keeps it where it was: siente → siéntese, golpea → golpéalo, quita → quítale. One-syllable dé + me is now usually written deme BPOC: déme.
Compare: Muévase (move!) ↔ No se mueva (don't move!)  ·  Míreme (look at me) ↔ No me mire (don't look at me)

“Let's” commands

The nosotros command means “let's ___”: Hablemos en otro cuarto = “Let's talk in another room.” It's formed like the usted command plus -mos (hable → hablemos).

Short commands with no verb

Some commands drop the verb entirely, just like English “Hands up!”: ¡Manos arriba! (hands above), ¡Alto! (halt), ¡Policía!. The instructor alternatives on the card work the same way: ¡Manos atrás! (hands behind), ¿Cinturón, sí o no?

5Him / Her / Me / Yourself — object and reflexive pronouns▾

Object pronouns

PronounMeaningBPOC examples
meme / to medéme (give me), no me mire (don't look at me)
lo / lahim / her (or it), receiving the action directlydesarmalo (disarm him), golpéalo / golpéala (beat him / her), pícalo (stab him)
leto him / to her / to you (usted) — the indirect objectpégale (hit him), dispárale (shoot at him), quítale la pistola (take the gun from him), ¿Dónde le duele? (where does it hurt you?), servirle (serve you)

The danger words in 26.3 show why this matters: the ending on the word tells you who the target is. Golpéalo is “beat him”; golpéala is “beat her.” If one suspect says it to another while looking at you, it's about you.

Reflexive verbs: “yourself” built into the verb

Many body-position commands are reflexive: the person does the action to themselves. English says “sit down”; Spanish says, in effect, “seat yourself.” The dictionary form ends in -se (sentarse), and the pronoun changes with the person:

WhoPronounsentarse (to sit down)
yomeme siento (I sit down)
tútete sientas
él / ella / ustedsese sienta
nosotrosnosnos sentamos
ellos / ustedessese sientan

So the usted command ends in -se: siéntese (sit down), levántese (get up), acúestese (lie down), cálmese (calm down), quédese (stay), bájese (get out/down), voltéese (turn around). The tú version ends in -te: siéntate, arráncate (take off!).

mover = to move something  →  No mueva la cabeza (don't move your head)
moverse = to move (yourself)  →  ¡No se mueva! (don't move!)

Body parts take “the,” not “your”

Spanish usually says “the head,” “the hands,” “the feet” where English says “your”: No mueva la cabeza, Cruce los pies, Deme la otra mano. Who it belongs to is already clear from the verb.

6Sentence Patterns English Doesn't Have▾

“Do you want me to call…?” — querer que + subjunctive

English can say “want me to call.” Spanish can't. It says “want that I call,” and the second verb goes into a special form called the subjunctive, used after wanting, asking, or needing someone else to do something. For most verbs it looks exactly like the usted command.

¿Quiere que llame una grúa?
Quiere (do you want) + que (that) + llame (I call — subjunctive of llamar) + una grúa (a tow truck)
Literally: “Do you want that I call a tow truck?”→ “Do you want me to call a tow truck?” (BPOC 26.6)

Compare ¿Quiere llamar a alguien? = “Do you want to call someone?” When the same person wants and does, Spanish uses the infinitive, just like English. Quiere que + subjunctive is only for wanting someone else to act.

“I'm going to…” — ir a + infinitive

Voy a llamar una ambulancia→ “I'm going to call an ambulance.” Same structure as English: voy (I go) + a (to) + infinitive. BPOC: Voy á llamar

“Where does it hurt?” — doler works backwards

Doler (to hurt) works like gustar (to please): the thing that hurts is the subject, and the person is the indirect object.

¿Dónde le duele?→ “Where does (it) hurt to you?” → “Where does it hurt?”
Me duele la cabeza = “My head hurts” (lit. “the head hurts me”). Me duelen las piernas = “My legs hurt” (plural verb, because legs is the subject).

“There is / there are” — hay

¿Hay alguien herido?→ “Is there someone injured?” → “Is anyone injured?” Hay covers both “there is” and “there are,” and doesn't change for plural.

Two verbs for “to be” — ser and estar

  • Ser = what or who something is (identity, role): ¿Quién es la víctima?, ¿Es usted el dueño del vehículo?
  • Estar = how or where something is right now (condition, location, result): ¿Está bien? (are you OK?), Está arrestado (you are under arrest).

“You are under arrest” uses estar because being arrested is a condition you're now in, not who you are.

“Was driving” — venir + gerund

¿Quién venía manejando?→ “Who was coming driving?” → “Who was driving?” Using venir (to come) instead of estar adds the sense of “on the way here,” which fits a crash. ¿Quién estaba manejando? also works.

“Were you wearing…?” — llevar puesto

¿Llevaba puesto el cinturón de seguridad?→ “Were you carrying put-on the belt of safety?” → “Were you wearing your seat belt?” Llevar = carry/wear, puesto = “put on,” and cinturón de seguridad = “belt of safety.”

The “personal a”

When the object of a verb is a person, Spanish puts a in front of it. It has no English translation.

¿Quiere que llame a alguien? (call someone) vs. ¿Quiere que llame una grúa? (call a tow truck — a thing, no a). ¿Quién llamó a la policía? treats the police as people. BPOC: á alquien

Contractions: al and del

Spanish has only two: a + el = al and de + el = del. Salga del vehículo (out of the vehicle); ¿Es usted el dueño del carro? (owner of the car).

“What happened” — lo que

¿Quién vio lo que pasó?→ “Who saw what happened?” Inside a sentence, “what” meaning “the thing that” is lo que, not qué.
7Nouns, Gender & Adjectives▾

Every noun is masculine or feminine

MasculineFeminine
“the” (one)ella
“the” (more than one)loslas
“a / an”ununa
Usual ending-o: el cuchillo, el carro-a: la pistola, la botella
  • Many nouns don't follow the -o/-a pattern and have to be learned with their article: el motor, la mano (hand, feminine despite -o), el pie / los pies (foot, masculine), la dirección, la licencia.
  • El arma: arma (weapon) is feminine, but because it starts with a stressed “a” sound it takes el in the singular to avoid “la arma.” The plural goes back to las armas. Suelte el arma is correct.
  • La policía = the police (the force); el policía / la policía = a police officer.
  • For people and many animals, the ending changes with the person: hijo / hija, novio / novia, dueño / dueña. The BPOC lists show the feminine in parentheses: golpéalo (golpéala), prometido (prometida).
  • A mixed group uses the masculine plural. Hijos = sons or children; padres = fathers or parents; hermanos = brothers or siblings.

Adjectives go after the noun and match it

el carro rojo (the red car) · la camisa roja (the red shirt) · los zapatos negros (the black shoes)
  • Adjectives ending in -o change to -a / -os / -as to match. Adjectives ending in -e or a consonant change only for plural: verde / verdes, azul / azules, gris / grises.
  • Some colors named after things don't change at all: café, naranja, beige (la camisa naranja, los pantalones café).
  • Matching applies to the person you're talking to, too: Quédese tranquilo to a man, tranquila to a woman; Está arrestado / arrestada.
  • “Of” phrases do the work of English compound nouns: cinturón de seguridad (seat belt), licencia de manejar (driver's license), lugar de nacimiento (place of birth), número de teléfono.

“Another” doesn't take “a”

Hablemos en otro cuarto = “Let's talk in another room” — never un otro. Likewise el otro vehículo = the other vehicle, la otra mano = the other hand.

8Questions — the 26.4 question words and how questions are built▾
  • Written questions open with ¿ and close with ?; exclamations use ¡ … !. They mark where the question starts, since the words alone often don't.
  • There's no “do / does / did” helper. A yes/no question is the statement with a rising voice, optionally with the subject moved after the verb: Usted habla inglés (you speak English) → ¿Habla usted inglés?
  • Question words always carry an accent mark, and the verb usually comes right after them.
SpanishEnglishNotes / BPOC use
¿Qué?What?¿En qué dirección iba? — “In what direction were you going?” Also “which” before a noun.
¿Cómo?How?Also what you say for “Pardon? What did you say?”
¿Por qué?Why?Lit. “for what?” The answer “because” is one word, porque.
¿Cuándo?When?
¿Dónde?Where?¿Dónde le duele?
¿Cuántos? / ¿Cuántas?How many?Matches the gender of what's counted: ¿Cuántos hombres? / ¿Cuántas personas?
¿Cuál? / ¿Cuáles?Which (one/ones)?Used for choosing from options; plural cuáles.
¿Quién? / ¿Quiénes?Who?¿Quién llamó a la policía?; plural quiénes for more than one person.
¿De quién? / ¿De quiénes?Whose?Lit. “of whom?” — Spanish has no ’s possessive: ¿De quién es el carro? = “Whose car is it?” (lit. “Of whom is the car?”)
¿A qué velocidad?How fast?Lit. “At what speed?” (BPOC 26.6)

12Self-Check — tap to reveal▾
Why is “Do you speak English?” ¿Habla usted inglés? and not ¿Hablas inglés? Answer
Both are correct Spanish. Hablas is the tú (informal) form; habla + usted is the formal form, which is what you use with the public. Usted takes the he/she verb form (section 2).
Turn Siéntese (sit down) into “Don't sit down.” Answer
No se siente. In a negative command the pronoun moves in front of the verb, and the accent goes away because the stress is back in its normal place (section 4).
A suspect yells ¡Quítale la pistola! to a friend. What's being said, and about whom? Answer
“Take the gun from him/her!” Quita = take away (tú command), le = from him/her — you, the officer. La pistola = the pistol.
How would you say “Do you want me to call your mother?” using the BPOC pattern? Answer
¿Quiere que llame a su madre? — quiere que + llame (subjunctive), the personal a because the mother is a person, and su = your (formal) (section 6).
Why is it el arma but la pistola, when both are feminine? Answer
Arma starts with a stressed “a,” so singular la changes to el to avoid “la arma.” It's still feminine: las armas, el arma blanca (section 7).
Fix the BPOC spelling: sepáre las pies. Answer
Separe los pies. Pie is masculine (el pie / los pies), and separe is already stressed on the right syllable (se-PA-re), so it takes no accent.
What tense is ¿En qué dirección iba usted? and why? Answer
The imperfect of ir: “Which way were you going?” It describes an action in progress at the moment of the crash (section 3).
How does ¿Dónde le duele? mean “Where does it hurt?” when le means “to you”? Answer
Doler works backwards from English: the body part is the subject and the person is the indirect object. Literally, “Where does (it) hurt to you?” (section 6).
Which of these is a tú command: suelte el arma or suelta el arma? Answer
Suelta is tú; suelte is usted. The BPOC card uses the tú form. Both mean “drop the weapon.”
Say “Look at me” (usted). Answer
Míreme. The positive command attaches me to the end and adds an accent to keep the stress on MI. The BPOC negative is No me mire.
