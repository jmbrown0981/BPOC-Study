Family Code 262.104 — Taking Possession of a Child in an Emergency Without a Court Order

← Hub

BPOC 19.5–19.6 — when an officer may take a child without a court order, what is not enough, what has to happen next (262.105–262.106), and who the child can be released to (CCP Art. 2A.058).

Family Code · Chapter 262 · Subchapter B

Three Gates Before
You Take the Child

Emergency removal without a court order is allowed only when all three are true. The statute says “under the following conditions, only” — the list is closed.

GATE 1 · TIME

No time for a court order

There is no time, consistent with the child’s health and safety, to get a temporary order, TRO, or attachment under Family Code 262.102(a).

AND
GATE 2 · WHO

An authorized person

A law enforcement officer, an authorized DFPS representative, or a juvenile probation officer.

AND
GATE 3 · GROUNDS

One listed condition

Facts that would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe one of the conditions on the next slide exists.

“Personal knowledge” vs. “corroborated information”: every condition is satisfied either by what you know first-hand, or by what someone else tells you that you have corroborated with your own personal knowledge. An uncorroborated report, by itself, never satisfies the statute.

Drug-use note: (a)(5) is written only for information from another person that you corroborate. If you see the drug use and the danger yourself, you are already under (a)(1) — personal knowledge of an immediate danger. Either way, drug use alone is not enough; it must be an immediate danger to the child. Meth manufacturing premises under (b) is the one ground that does not require a separate immediate-danger finding.
STATUTE TEXT — 262.104(a)–(b)

(a) If there is no time to obtain a temporary order, temporary restraining order, or attachment under Section 262.102(a) before taking possession of a child consistent with the health and safety of that child, an authorized representative of the Department of Family and Protective Services, a law enforcement officer, or a juvenile probation officer may take possession of a child without a court order under the following conditions, only:

(1) on personal knowledge of facts that would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that there is an immediate danger to the physical health or safety of the child;

(2) on information furnished by another that has been corroborated by personal knowledge of facts and all of which taken together would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that there is an immediate danger to the physical health or safety of the child;

(3) on personal knowledge of facts that would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that the child has been the victim of sexual abuse or of trafficking under Section 20A.02 or 20A.03, Penal Code;

(4) on information furnished by another that has been corroborated by personal knowledge of facts and all of which taken together would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that the child has been the victim of sexual abuse or of trafficking under Section 20A.02 or 20A.03, Penal Code; or

(5) on information furnished by another that has been corroborated by personal knowledge of facts and all of which taken together would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that the parent or person who has possession of the child is currently using a controlled substance as defined by Chapter 481, Health and Safety Code, and the use constitutes an immediate danger to the physical health or safety of the child.

(b) An authorized representative of the Department of Family and Protective Services, a law enforcement officer, or a juvenile probation officer may take possession of a child under Subsection (a) on personal knowledge or information furnished by another, that has been corroborated by personal knowledge, that would lead a person of ordinary prudence and caution to believe that the parent or person who has possession of the child has permitted the child to remain on premises used for the manufacture of methamphetamine.

(c)

Limits on the authority

What is not enough

An uncorroborated report

A call, a neighbor, or an anonymous tip by itself. You need to corroborate it with your own personal knowledge of facts before it counts. (a)(2), (4), (5) · (b)

A remote medical opinion

The opinion of a medical professional under contract with DFPS who did not physically examine the child cannot be the sole basis for removal. (c)

There was time to get an order

If the child’s safety allows time to get a temporary order, TRO, or attachment under 262.102(a), this section does not apply — the court order route does. (a) lead-in

Also not a ground by itself

  • Drug use by the parent with no immediate danger to the child.
  • Neglect that is not an immediate danger. (A judge may consider neglect for an emergency order under 262.102, but 262.104 lists only the conditions on the previous slide.)
  • A dirty or unsafe home that is not an immediate danger to the child’s physical health or safety.

Write it so the affidavit holds up

  • The suit filed after removal must be backed by an affidavit stating the facts that met one of these conditions, based on personal knowledge or corroborated information. 262.105(b)
  • It must also show removal was necessary: staying home was contrary to the child’s welfare, there was no time for a full hearing, and reasonable efforts were made to avoid removal.
  • Your report is where those facts come from: what you saw, who told you what, and how you verified it.
STATUTE TEXT — 262.104(c)

(c) An authorized representative of the Department of Family and Protective Services, a law enforcement officer, or a juvenile probation officer may not take possession of a child under Subsection (a) based solely on the opinion of a medical professional under contract with the Department of Family and Protective Services who did not conduct a physical examination of the child.

262.105 places these filing duties on “the person taking the child into possession.” Officers normally release the child to DFPS (below), and DFPS carries the case forward — follow department policy on the hand-off.

CCP Art. 2A.058(a) — release the child to

  • a DFPS-licensed residential child-care facility authorized by DFPS to take possession,
  • a juvenile probation department,
  • DFPS, or
  • any other person authorized by law to take possession of the child.

2A.058(b) — before releasing to a non-governmental person

  • NCIC: confirm the child is not a missing child.
  • NCIC: confirm the person has no protective order against them and is not a registered sex offender (unless the child’s parent or guardian with no contact restrictions), plus other info DFPS considers relevant.
  • Call the DFPS Texas Abuse Hotline to check the abuse/neglect registry.
  • Verify the person is at least 18.
  • Keep a record: the child’s name or pseudonyms, and the name and address of the person receiving the child.
STATUTE TEXT — FC 262.105(a) · 262.106(a), (c) · CCP 2A.058

Family Code 262.105(a) When a child is taken into possession without a court order, the person taking the child into possession, without unnecessary delay, shall:

(1) file a suit affecting the parent-child relationship;

(2) request the court to appoint an attorney ad litem for the child; and

(3) request an initial hearing to be held by no later than the first business day after the date the child is taken into possession.

Family Code 262.106(a) The court in which a suit has been filed after a child has been taken into possession without a court order by a governmental entity shall hold an initial hearing on or before the first business day after the date the child is taken into possession. The court shall render orders that are necessary to protect the physical health and safety of the child. If the court is unavailable for a hearing on the first business day, then, and only in that event, the hearing shall be held no later than the first business day after the court becomes available, provided that the hearing is held no later than the third business day after the child is taken into possession.

(c) If the initial hearing is not held within the time required, the child shall be returned to the parent, managing conservator, possessory conservator, guardian, caretaker, or custodian who is presently entitled to possession of the child.

Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 2A.058. (a) A law enforcement officer who takes possession of a child under Section 262.104, Family Code, may release the child to:

(1) a residential child-care facility licensed by the Department of Family and Protective Services under Chapter 42, Human Resources Code, if the facility is authorized by the department to take possession of the child;

(2) a juvenile probation department;

(3) the Department of Family and Protective Services; or

(4) any other person authorized by law to take possession of the child.

(b) Before a law enforcement officer may release a child to a person authorized by law to take possession of the child other than a governmental entity, the officer must:

(1) verify with the National Crime Information Center that the child is not a missing child;

(2) search the relevant databases of the National Crime Information Center system, including those pertaining to protection orders, historical protection orders, warrants, sex offender registries, and persons on supervised release to:

(A) verify that the person to whom the child is being released: (i) does not have a protective order issued against the person; and (ii) is not registered as a sex offender unless the person is the child's parent or guardian and there are no restrictions regarding the person's contact with the child; and

(B) obtain any other information the Department of Family and Protective Services considers: (i) relevant to protect the welfare of the child; or (ii) reflective of the responsibility of the person to whom the child is being released;

(3) call the Department of Family and Protective Services Texas Abuse Hotline to determine whether the person to whom the child is being released is listed in the registry as a person who abused or neglected a child;

(4) verify that the person to whom the child is being released is at least 18 years of age; and

(5) maintain a record regarding the child's placement, including: (A) identifying information about the child, including the child's name or pseudonyms; and (B) the name and address of the person to whom the child is being released.

Scenario checks

An anonymous caller says a mother is high and her toddler is in danger. You arrive; the mother answers the door coherent, and the child appears unharmed. Can you take the child under 262.104? Answer
No. The tip is not corroborated by anything you personally observed, and nothing you saw shows current drug use that is an immediate danger. Uncorroborated information never satisfies (a)(5). Document what you saw and make the DFPS report.
Same call, but inside you see the mother passed out next to a pipe, and the 2-year-old is alone next to an open, filled bathtub. Which condition applies? Answer
(a)(5) — the caller’s information, corroborated by your personal knowledge, shows current drug use that is an immediate danger. Your own observations also support (a)(1). Either one works if there is no time to get a court order.
During a CSCAL encounter, a 12-year-old tells you a relative has been sexually abusing her, and her account matches injuries you can see. She lives with that relative. Answer
(a)(4) — information from another (the child) corroborated by your personal knowledge (the visible injuries) that the child has been a victim of sexual abuse. A sexual-abuse ground does not need a separate immediate-danger finding.
On a narcotics search warrant you find an active meth cook in the kitchen and a 6-year-old asleep in the back bedroom. Answer
(b) — personal knowledge that the person in possession let the child remain on premises used to manufacture methamphetamine. It also likely supports (a)(1) (immediate danger), but (b) does not require that finding.
A DFPS-contracted doctor reviews photos and says the bruises look like abuse, but never examined the child. Nothing else has been verified. Can that alone support removal? Answer
No. 262.104(c) bars removal based solely on the opinion of a DFPS-contracted medical professional who did not physically examine the child. You would need other facts that meet one of the (a) or (b) conditions.
You take a child under 262.104. The child’s adult aunt arrives and offers to take her. What must you do before releasing the child to the aunt? Answer
CCP 2A.058(b): NCIC check that the child is not missing; NCIC checks that the aunt has no protective order against her and is not a registered sex offender; call the DFPS Texas Abuse Hotline registry; verify she is at least 18; and keep a record of the child’s identity and the aunt’s name and address. Releasing to DFPS, a juvenile probation department, or a licensed facility does not require these checks.
