Probable Cause & Reasonable Suspicion

← Guide Index

The foundational standards for when an officer can search, and when an officer can briefly detain — and how the two standards differ.

Carroll Beck Gates Sokolow
1925 CARROLL v. UNITED STATES ▾

Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132 (1925)

The automobile exception — probable cause substitutes for a warrant because a car can be driven away.

Facts: Federal prohibition agents had earlier negotiated with the defendants to buy whiskey, and later recognized the same car making a run on a known bootlegging route between Detroit and Grand Rapids. They stopped and searched it without a warrant and found 68 quarts of concealed liquor.

Holding: An officer with probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband may search it without a warrant, because the vehicle's mobility makes getting a warrant first impracticable — the "automobile exception."

On patrol: probable cause to search a car does not require a warrant the way probable cause to search a house usually does. The vehicle's ability to simply drive away is what does the constitutional work.
1964 BECK v. OHIO ▾

Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89 (1964)

Sincerely believing you have probable cause isn't the same as having it.

Facts: Officers, relying on vague, unspecified "information" and their own knowledge that the defendant had a prior gambling record, stopped and arrested him without a warrant, searched his car and found nothing, then found evidence on his person at the station.

Holding: Good faith on the part of arresting officers is not enough — probable cause is judged by an objective standard based on the facts actually known at the time, not by how genuinely the officer believed it. Without that, Fourth Amendment protection would simply "evaporate."

On patrol: "I really believed it" is not a substitute for being able to articulate the specific, objective facts that added up to probable cause.
1983 ILLINOIS v. GATES ▾

Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983)

An anonymous tip, corroborated by real police work, can add up to probable cause.

Facts: An anonymous letter to police alleged the Gateses were drug traffickers and predicted a detailed, specific travel plan for picking up drugs. Officers independently corroborated several predicted details (a flight reservation, the travel pattern) before a search turned up drugs and contraband.

Holding: Courts should evaluate probable cause using the "totality of the circumstances" rather than the old rigid two-pronged Aguilar-Spinelli test (which required separately proving an informant's "basis of knowledge" and "veracity"). Independent police corroboration of an anonymous tip's specific predicted details carries real weight. Probable cause is "a practical, non-technical conception" — something "reasonable and prudent men (peace officers), not legal technicians (attorneys)," act on.

On patrol: an anonymous, unverifiable tip alone is thin — but if you go out and independently confirm the specific things the tip predicted, that corroboration is exactly what the totality-of-the-circumstances test rewards.
1989 U.S. v. SOKOLOW ▾

United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1 (1989)

Individually-innocent facts can add up to reasonable suspicion — and calling it a "profile" doesn't weaken that.

Facts: DEA agents stopped a traveler at the airport who had paid cash for an expensive round-trip ticket under a name not matching his phone contact, checked no luggage for a long trip, spent an unusually short time at his destination, and appeared nervous. A search of his carry-on found over a kilogram of cocaine.

Holding: Reasonable suspicion may rest on a combination of factors that, taken together, add up to reasonable suspicion — even though each factor alone is consistent with innocent travel, and even though the combination happened to match an informal DEA "drug courier profile." Reasonable suspicion is considerably less than proof by a preponderance of the evidence, and less than probable cause.

On patrol: you don't need one damning fact — several individually-innocent observations, viewed together, can clear the reasonable-suspicion bar for a brief investigative stop.
