Arrest & Home Entry

← Guide Index

The Fourth Amendment's firmest line: the threshold of a home. When can officers cross it without a warrant, and whose home matters.

Payton Steagald Santana
1980 PAYTON v. NEW YORK ▾

Payton v. New York, 445 U.S. 573 (1980)

No warrantless, forced entry into a suspect's own home for a routine felony arrest — absent exigent circumstances.

Facts: With probable cause that Payton had committed murder, six NYPD officers forced open the door of his Bronx apartment without any warrant and entered to arrest him, seizing a shell casing later used as evidence.

Holding: Absent exigent circumstances, the Fourth Amendment (via the Fourteenth) prohibits police from making a warrantless, nonconsensual entry into a suspect's home to make a routine felony arrest. An arrest warrant — not a search warrant — is enough, provided officers have reason to believe the suspect is home at the time.

Citation note: some study materials cite this case as "100 US 1371" — that's actually the parallel Supreme Court Reporter citation (100 S. Ct. 1371), not the U.S. Reports citation. The correct citation is 445 U.S. 573, 100 S. Ct. 1371, 63 L. Ed. 2d 639 (1980).
1981 STEAGALD v. UNITED STATES ▾

Steagald v. United States, 451 U.S. 204 (1981)

An arrest warrant for the suspect doesn't authorize entry into someone else's home to look for him.

Facts: DEA agents, armed only with an arrest warrant for a fugitive (not a search warrant), entered Gary Steagald's home looking for him. The fugitive wasn't there, but agents saw what they believed was cocaine and, after further searches, found 43 pounds of it — leading to Steagald's own prosecution.

Holding: Absent consent or exigent circumstances, an arrest warrant alone is not sufficient to search for its subject inside a third party's home — a separate search warrant for that home is required. This extends Payton's home-entry protection to third parties whose homes are searched for someone else's arrest.

Citation note: commonly mis-cited as "101 US. 1642" — that's the parallel S. Ct. citation (101 S. Ct. 1642), not the U.S. Reports citation. The correct citation is 451 U.S. 204, 101 S. Ct. 1642, 68 L. Ed. 2d 38 (1981).
Payton vs. Steagald: an arrest warrant is enough to enter the named suspect's own home (Payton) — but entering someone else's home to look for that same suspect needs a search warrant for that different location (Steagald).
1976 UNITED STATES v. SANTANA ▾

United States v. Santana, 427 U.S. 38 (1976)

Retreating into your own home doesn't defeat an arrest that already started in public — a doorway counts as public.

Facts: Officers approached Santana, who was standing in her open doorway holding a paper bag containing marked "buy money" from a controlled heroin purchase. As officers approached, she retreated into her home's vestibule, where they caught her; heroin fell from the bag as she tried to get away.

Holding: By retreating into a private space, a suspect cannot defeat an otherwise proper arrest already set in motion in a public place. Standing in an open doorway is itself a "public place" for Fourth Amendment purposes, since there's no expectation of privacy there — and the need to act quickly justified continuing the arrest across the threshold without a warrant ("hot pursuit").

On patrol: if an arrest is validly underway while the suspect is somewhere public — including an open doorway — a retreat inside doesn't require officers to stop and get a warrant.
