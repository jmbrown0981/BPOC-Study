The current, controlling Fourth Amendment standard for evaluating excessive-force claims — decided May 2025.

Barnes v. Felix
2025 BARNES v. FELIX ▾

Barnes v. Felix, 605 U.S. ___ (2025), 145 S. Ct. 1353

The Supreme Court unanimously rejected the narrow "moment-of-threat" test for excessive force — the full totality of circumstances controls.

Facts: An officer stopped Ashtian Barnes for a suspected toll violation and ordered him out of the car. As Barnes began driving away, the officer jumped onto the moving car's doorsill and fired two shots through the windshield within about two seconds of stepping onto the sill, fatally wounding Barnes. The whole encounter, from the car starting to move to stopping, lasted about five seconds.

Holding: The lower courts had applied a "moment-of-threat" rule, asking only whether the officer reasonably feared for his safety in the narrow window right before firing. The Supreme Court unanimously rejected that narrow framing: evaluating whether force was excessive under the Fourth Amendment requires looking at the totality of the circumstances — including events leading up to the moment force was used, not just a frozen snapshot of the final seconds.

Very recent — verify before relying on it operationally: this case was decided May 15, 2025. The full U.S. Reports citation isn't finalized yet (it will eventually replace the "605 U.S. ___" placeholder); the S. Ct. citation, 145 S. Ct. 1353, is stable. Departmental use-of-force policy updates in response to this decision should be confirmed with current agency guidance rather than this guide alone.
On patrol: if a use-of-force incident is ever reviewed, the analysis won't stop at "was I in danger in the last two seconds" — it will look at the whole sequence of events, including choices the officer made earlier that led up to that moment (like whether stepping onto a moving car's doorsill was itself part of what created the danger).
