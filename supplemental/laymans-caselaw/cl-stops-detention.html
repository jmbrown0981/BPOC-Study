Stops, Detention & the Three Tiers of Encounter

← Guide Index

The line between a consensual encounter, an investigatory stop, and an arrest — and when a warrantless arrest is valid outside a home.

Terry Royer Mendenhall Dyar
1968 TERRY v. OHIO ▾

Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968)

The origin of "stop and frisk" — reasonable suspicion for the stop, a separate reasonable suspicion for the frisk.

Facts: An officer, based on his own experience, watched two men repeatedly walk past a store window and confer in a way he recognized as consistent with "casing a job, a stick-up." He stopped them, identified himself, and — believing they might be armed — patted down their outer clothing, finding a concealed weapon.

Holding: Police may briefly stop a person based on reasonable suspicion that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime, and may frisk for weapons if there's a separate reasonable suspicion the person is armed and dangerous — without a warrant and without full probable cause. The officer's suspicion must rest on specific, articulable facts, not an inchoate hunch.

On patrol: Terry is the foundation of the middle tier between a consensual chat and a full arrest — the investigatory stop. The frisk that goes with it is limited to a weapons pat-down, not a general search.
1983 FLORIDA v. ROYER ▾

Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983)

Retaining someone's ID and moving them to a closed room can turn a consensual stop into an arrest.

Facts: Detectives approached a traveler at an airport matching a "drug courier profile," took his ticket and ID, told him he was suspected of narcotics trafficking, and moved him to a small room — retrieving his luggage without consent — before he produced a key to open it, revealing drugs.

Holding: The initial consensual encounter escalated into a de facto arrest once officers retained his documents and moved him to an enclosed room without telling him he was free to leave — at that point, probable cause was required, and the detectives didn't yet have it.

On patrol: the combination of keeping someone's ID and relocating them without saying "you're free to go" is what tips a stop into an arrest — not any one factor alone.
1980 U.S. v. MENDENHALL ▾

United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544 (1980)

The "free to leave" test for deciding whether a seizure has occurred at all.

Facts: DEA agents approached a traveler at an airport, asked for her ticket and ID, noticed a name discrepancy, and — after she became visibly nervous — asked her to accompany them to their office, which she voluntarily did. She was later asked for consent to a search and told she could decline.

Holding: A person is "seized" under the Fourth Amendment only if, under the totality of the circumstances, a reasonable person in their position would have believed they were not free to leave. Because nothing coercive happened here beyond asking questions and voluntary compliance, no seizure occurred.

Compare to Royer: same airport-stop setting, opposite outcome — Mendenhall found no seizure because the encounter stayed voluntary; Royer found a seizure once documents were retained and the traveler was relocated without being told he could leave.
2003 DYAR v. STATE ▾

Dyar v. State, 125 S.W.3d 460 (Tex. Crim. App. 2003)

A Texas case on when a location the suspect ends up at — not just the original scene — counts as a "suspicious place."

Facts: The defendant was the sole occupant of a one-car accident and had already been taken to the hospital before police arrived at the crash scene. At the hospital, officers developed grounds to believe he'd been driving while intoxicated and arrested him without a warrant.

Holding: The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals held the totality of the circumstances reasonably showed the defendant was found in a "suspicious place" under CCP Art. 14.03(a)(1) — even though that place was the hospital, not the original accident scene — supporting the warrantless arrest.

On patrol: "suspicious place" isn't limited to the original crime scene. If the totality of circumstances connects the person to a suspected offense wherever they're found, that location can qualify.
