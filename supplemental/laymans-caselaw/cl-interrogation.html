Custodial Interrogation & Confessions

← Guide Index

When the warnings are required, when they expire, and what makes a statement voluntary once they've been given.

Miranda Shatzer Creager
1966 MIRANDA v. ARIZONA ▾

Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966)

The four warnings required before custodial interrogation.

Facts: The case addressed the inherently coercive pressures of custodial interrogation and whether suspects must be told of their rights before being questioned in custody.

Holding: Before questioning a suspect in custody, an officer must warn them: (1) they have the right to remain silent; (2) anything said can be used against them in court; (3) they have the right to an attorney; and (4) if they can't afford one, an attorney will be appointed before questioning if they want one. These warnings apply only when custody and interrogation occur together — their purpose is to dispel the coercion inherent in custodial surroundings so any statement is truly voluntary.

On patrol: a voluntary, non-custodial conversation — where the person is free to end it and leave — doesn't trigger Miranda, no matter the topic. It's the combination of custody + interrogation that matters.
2010 MARYLAND v. SHATZER ▾

Maryland v. Shatzer, 559 U.S. 98 (2010)

Once a suspect invokes the right to counsel, a 14-day break in custody resets the clock.

Facts: Shatzer, already incarcerated on an unrelated offense, invoked his right to counsel during a 2003 jail interview about alleged child abuse. The investigation closed and he returned to the general prison population. In 2006, new evidence prompted a second interrogation, where he waived his rights and made incriminating statements.

Holding: A break in custody of 14 days or more ends the Edwards presumption against re-interrogation (the rule that once a suspect invokes the right to counsel, police can't re-initiate questioning). Fourteen days gives the suspect time to get reacclimated to normal life and consult with friends or counsel — even returning to the general prison population (without full release) counts as a "break in custody" for this purpose.

Citation note: often mis-cited as "130 US. 1213" — that's the parallel S. Ct. citation, not the U.S. Reports citation. The correct citation is 559 U.S. 98, 130 S. Ct. 1213, 175 L. Ed. 2d 1045 (2010).
1997 CREAGER v. STATE ▾

Creager v. State, 952 S.W.2d 852 (Tex. Crim. App. 1997)

A Texas case on what makes a custodial statement voluntary — and a specific warning defect that can sink it.

Facts: The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals reviewed whether the defendant's written custodial statement was voluntary, examining the circumstances under which it was taken and the warning language used before the interview.

Holding: Voluntariness depends on the totality of the circumstances; a confession is involuntary if those circumstances show the defendant's will was "overborne" by police coercion. A warning that a statement can be used "for or against" the suspect — rather than simply "against" — is itself an impropriety that can, by itself, make the resulting statement inadmissible, because it misleadingly suggests the statement could help the suspect.

On patrol: get the warning language right. Telling a suspect their statement can be used "for or against" them isn't a more complete warning — it's a legally defective one under Texas law.
