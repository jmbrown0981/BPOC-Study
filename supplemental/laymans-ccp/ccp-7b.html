Art. 7B.001–.003 APPLYING FOR & ISSUING A PROTECTIVE ORDER (SEXUAL/STALKING/TRAFFICKING/BURGLARY VICTIMS) ▾

Art. 7B.001 – APPLICATION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

Unlike family violence protective orders, these don't require any relationship between victim and offender.

A protective order under this subchapter can be requested — with no relationship requirement between the applicant and the alleged offender — by the victim of specific offenses: trafficking of persons, continuous trafficking, sexual assault of a child, indecency with a child, sexual assault, aggravated sexual assault, stalking, indecent assault, or certain second-degree-or-worse burglaries of a habitation. A parent/guardian may apply on behalf of a victim under 18 or an adult ward, and the prosecutor may apply on the victim's behalf. Following a conviction or deferred adjudication for a listed offense, the prosecutor generally must file the application unless the victim asks them not to (this override doesn't apply to minor or ward victims). The application must be filed in the county where the applicant or offender lives, or where the offense occurred.

Art. 7B.002 – TEMPORARY EX PARTE ORDER

Emergency protection issued before the offender even gets notice.

If the court finds a clear and present danger of the offense (or other harm) from the information in the application, it may issue a temporary ex parte order — without notifying the alleged offender and without a hearing — to protect the applicant or a family/household member. A military protective order issued because the person was a reported sexual assault victim is by itself sufficient to establish that danger.

Art. 7B.003 – REQUIRED FINDINGS; ISSUANCE OF PROTECTIVE ORDER

After a full hearing, the court must find whether there are reasonable grounds to believe the applicant was the victim of a listed offense; if so, it issues the protective order. A conviction or deferred adjudication for the offense automatically satisfies "reasonable grounds."

Art. 7B.004–.005 CHILD HEARSAY & PROTECTIVE ORDER CONDITIONS ▾

Art. 7B.004 – HEARSAY STATEMENT OF CHILD VICTIM

A child victim's out-of-court statement can come in at the protective order hearing.

At a hearing on one of these applications, a statement made by a child under 14 describing certain sexual or trafficking offenses committed against them is admissible, under the same rules that let a child's abuse statement into a Family Code custody proceeding.

Art. 7B.005 – CONDITIONS SPECIFIED BY PROTECTIVE ORDER

What a judge can actually order the offender to do or not do.

The court may order the alleged offender to take specific protective action, or prohibit them from:

  • Communicating with the applicant or family/household member in a threatening or harassing manner (or at all, except through an attorney, for good cause).
  • Going near the applicant's home, work, school, or child-care facility.
  • Engaging in conduct — including following — likely to harass, annoy, alarm, abuse, torment, or embarrass the applicant.
  • Possessing a firearm (unless the offender is an on-duty peace officer).
  • Tracking or monitoring the applicant's property or vehicle without consent, including via tracking apps/devices or physical surveillance.

If a stay-away distance is included, the order must specifically describe the prohibited location and minimum distance. The court can also suspend the offender's handgun license.

Art. 7B.006–.008 WARNING LANGUAGE, DURATION & RESCISSION ▾

Art. 7B.006 – WARNING ON PROTECTIVE ORDER

The mandatory boilerplate warning language every order must display.

Every order (including a temporary ex parte order) must prominently state that violation can mean contempt — up to a $500 fine and/or six months in jail — and that no one, including the protected person, can authorize ignoring the order. It must also state that anyone other than an on-duty peace officer who's subject to the order may not possess a firearm or ammunition. Final orders (not ex parte ones) must additionally warn that violating the order by committing a prohibited act can carry up to a $4,000 fine and/or one year in jail, separate from any charge for the underlying act itself.

Art. 7B.007 – DURATION OF PROTECTIVE ORDER; RESCISSION

An order lasts for the stated period, or until the second anniversary of issuance if no period is stated — up to the lifetime of both parties if the court so orders. A lifetime order is mandatory if the offender is convicted (or placed on deferred adjudication) for a listed offense and required to register as a sex offender for life. An adult victim, a parent/guardian of a minor/ward victim, or the original applicant may apply to rescind the order at any time (a parent/guardian who is themselves the offender cannot).

Art. 7B.008 – APPLICATION OF OTHER LAW

Except where this subchapter says otherwise, Family Code Title 4 (the general protective-order framework) fills in any gaps.

Art. 7B.051 STALKING PROTECTIVE ORDER — REQUEST ▾

Art. 7B.051 – REQUEST FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

A separate, streamlined path to a protective order specifically tied to a pending stalking case.

At any proceeding related to a stalking offense (PC §42.072) where the defendant appears before the court, the victim may request a protective order under Family Code Title 4 procedures. The request is made by filing an application the same way a Title 4 application is filed. The court issues the order if it finds probable cause the stalking offense was committed and that the defendant's pattern of conduct indicates future prohibited conduct is likely (Art. 7B.052) — enforcement then follows Title 4 procedure end to end (Art. 7B.053).

Art. 7B.101 PROTECTIVE ORDER — OFFENSE MOTIVATED BY BIAS OR PREJUDICE ▾

Art. 7B.101 – REQUEST FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

A protective order path for hate-crime-adjacent offenses.

A person may request a Title 4 protective order at any proceeding in county or district court related to a Penal Code Title 5 offense (offenses against the person) or certain criminal mischief/graffiti offenses (PC §28.02, 28.03, 28.08), where it's alleged the defendant committed the offense because of bias or prejudice as described in Article 42.014 (Texas's hate-crime enhancement provision). As with the stalking order, the court issues it on a finding of probable cause plus a bias/prejudice finding, in lieu of the standard family-violence finding.
