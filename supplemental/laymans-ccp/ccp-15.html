Chapter 15: Arrest Under Warrant

← Guide Index

BPOC 10.10–10.11 — how an arrest warrant and complaint are built, how the warrant gets executed, and the magistration duties every arrestee is owed within 48 hours. Cited in BPOC Chapter 10 (Arrest, Search & Seizure) and Chapter 7 (Interview & Interrogation) for the Art. 15.17 rights warnings. This is the statute the "48-hour rule" and the right-to-counsel/right-to-remain-silent magistration warnings actually come from.

Art. 15.01–.02 Art. 15.03–.05 Art. 15.06–.07 Art. 15.16 Art. 15.17 Art. 15.18–.21 Art. 15.22–.26 Art. 15.27 Art. 15.171
How to read this chapter: Chapter 15 covers the warrant path to arrest — how a warrant and its supporting complaint are built (Art. 15.01–.07), how it's executed (Art. 15.16, 15.22–.26), and what happens to the person afterward (Art. 15.17–.21, 15.27, 15.171). Warrantless arrest authority lives in Chapter 14, not here — but Art. 15.17's magistration duties (the 48-hour rule, the counsel/silence warnings) apply to every arrest, warrant or no warrant, so it's the single most-tested article in this chapter regardless of how the arrest started.
Art. 15.01–.02 WARRANT OF ARREST & REQUISITES OF WARRANT ▾

Art. 15.01 – WARRANT OF ARREST

The definition: a written order from a magistrate telling an officer (or a specially named person) to bring the accused in.

A "warrant of arrest" is a written order from a magistrate, directed to a peace officer or some other specially named person, commanding that person to take the body of the accused and deal with them according to law.

Art. 15.02 – REQUISITES OF WARRANT

Three substantial requirements — form doesn't matter as long as the substance is there.

A warrant issues in the name of "The State of Texas" and is sufficient, regardless of its exact form, if it has these three substantial requisites:

  • Identifies the accused — by name if known, or by a reasonably definite description if not.
  • Names the offense — states that the person is accused of some offense against state law, naming it.
  • Signed by the magistrate — with the magistrate's office named either in the body of the warrant or with the signature.
Compare to Art. 15.05: the warrant's requisites and the complaint's requisites (below) are a matched pair — the complaint is the sworn accusation, the warrant is the magistrate's order acting on it. Both use the same "substance over form" standard.
Art. 15.03–.05 MAGISTRATE MAY ISSUE WARRANT OR SUMMONS; COMPLAINT; REQUISITES OF COMPLAINT ▾

Art. 15.03 – MAGISTRATE MAY ISSUE WARRANT OR SUMMONS

When a magistrate can act, and the lesser-known alternative to a warrant — the summons.

A magistrate may issue a warrant of arrest (or a summons) in any case where the magistrate is authorized by law to verbally order an arrest, when someone swears an oath before the magistrate that another person has committed an offense, or in any case this Code specially authorizes.

A summons can issue anywhere a warrant could, in the same form, except it summons the defendant to appear at a stated time and place instead of ordering an immediate arrest. It's served personally, by leaving it at the defendant's dwelling with someone of suitable age and discretion, or by mail to the defendant's last known address. If the defendant doesn't show up in response, a warrant is issued.

The oath supporting a warrant or summons request doesn't require an in-person appearance — a person may appear before the magistrate through a two-way audio/video "electronic broadcast system" (including secure internet videoconferencing). A recording of that communication must be made and preserved until the defendant is acquitted or all appeals are exhausted; defense counsel can get a copy on payment of reasonable reproduction cost.

Art. 15.04 – COMPLAINT

The name for the sworn charging affidavit.

The affidavit made before a magistrate, district attorney, or county attorney is called a "complaint" if it charges the commission of an offense.

Art. 15.05 – REQUISITES OF COMPLAINT

Four substantial requirements, mirroring Art. 15.02's approach to warrants.

A complaint is sufficient, regardless of exact form, if it has these four substantial requisites:

  • States the accused's name, or a reasonably definite description if unknown.
  • Shows the accused committed an offense against state law — either directly, or that the affiant has good reason to believe (and does believe) the accused committed it.
  • States the time and place of the offense, as definitely as the affiant can manage.
  • Is signed by the affiant, by name or mark.
Art. 15.06–.07 WARRANT EXTENDS TO EVERY PART OF THE STATE; WARRANT ISSUED BY OTHER MAGISTRATE ▾

Art. 15.06 – WARRANT EXTENDS TO EVERY PART OF THE STATE

A warrant from a county/district clerk or magistrate is good statewide — with one exception.

A warrant of arrest issued by any county or district clerk, or by any magistrate except a mayor of an incorporated city or town, extends to every part of the state. Any peace officer to whom it's directed — or into whose hands it's transferred — may execute it in any county.

Art. 15.07 – WARRANT ISSUED BY OTHER MAGISTRATE

The mayor-warrant exception, and how to fix it: endorsement.

A warrant issued by a mayor can't be executed outside the issuing county unless it's endorsed:

  • By a judge of a court of record — then it's good statewide ("Let this warrant be executed in any county of the State of Texas").
  • By any magistrate in the county where the accused is found — then it's good in that county only ("Let this warrant be executed in the county of ..........").

The endorsement must be dated and officially signed by the magistrate making it.

Art. 15.16 HOW WARRANT IS EXECUTED ▾

Art. 15.16 – HOW WARRANT IS EXECUTED

Where the arrestee goes right after the arrest — and the escape hatch that lets an officer prioritize the Art. 15.17 warnings.

The officer executing a warrant of arrest must, without unnecessary delay, take (or have taken) the arrested person before the magistrate who issued the warrant or the magistrate named in it — if that magistrate is in the same county as the arrest. If the issuing/named magistrate is in another county, the person must instead be taken before some magistrate in the county of arrest without unnecessary delay.

The (b) override: notwithstanding that rule, the officer may instead take the arrestee before a magistrate in a different county if that gets them the Art. 15.17 warnings faster. Speed of magistration beats venue convenience.
Art. 15.17 DUTIES OF ARRESTING OFFICER AND MAGISTRATE ▾

Art. 15.17 – DUTIES OF ARRESTING OFFICER AND MAGISTRATE

The single most important article in this chapter: the 48-hour magistration rule and the full menu of warnings every arrestee must receive.

The 48-hour rule. The person making the arrest (or the person with custody of the arrestee) must, without unnecessary delay and not later than 48 hours after the arrest, take the arrestee — or present them by videoconference — before a magistrate of the county of arrest, or before a magistrate in any other county if that delivers the warnings faster (including the county that issued the warrant). This duty applies to every arrest, not just warrant arrests — it's why Chapter 14 (warrantless arrest) and this chapter both point here.

Subsection (a) also covers a special case: if the arrestee is civilly committed as a sexually violent predator under Health & Safety Code Ch. 841 and resides at a civil commitment facility, the magistrate may perform these duties at that facility.

What the magistrate must tell the arrestee (in clear language, in person or by videoconference):

  • The accusation against them, and any affidavit filed with it.
  • The right to retain counsel.
  • The right to remain silent.
  • The right to have an attorney present during any interview with officers or prosecutors.
  • The right to terminate an interview at any time.
  • The right to an examining trial.
  • The right to request appointed counsel if indigent, plus the procedures for requesting it — the magistrate must ensure reasonable assistance completing the paperwork.
  • If applicable, the right to file the affidavit described in Art. 17.028(f) (relevant to certain bail decisions).
  • That they are not required to make a statement, and any statement they do make can be used against them.

If the arrestee doesn't speak/understand English or is deaf, the magistrate must inform them consistent with Art. 38.30/38.31 (interpreter requirements) — see also subsection (c) below.

Ties directly to Art. 1.051: if the arrestee is indigent, requests counsel, and the magistrate is authorized under Art. 26.04 to appoint counsel in that county, the magistrate appoints counsel under Art. 1.051's framework right then. If the magistrate isn't authorized to appoint, the request forms must be transmitted to the county's appointing authority within 24 hours. This is the appointment clock Art. 1.051 sets in motion.

The magistrate must also determine whether the arrestee is currently on bail for a separate offense (relevant to Art. 17.027) and, after allowing reasonable time to consult counsel, admit the arrestee to bail if bail is allowed by law. A record of the magistrate/arrestee communication must be made and preserved until the earlier of the pretrial hearing's end, or 91 days (misdemeanor) / 120 days (felony) after the record was made. Defense counsel can obtain a copy on payment of reasonable reproduction cost.

Other subsections worth knowing:

  • (a-1) — if the magistrate has credible information a person brought before them may have a mental illness or intellectual disability, the magistrate must conduct the Art. 16.22 or 17.032 proceedings.
  • (a-2) — on presenting a person arrested for an Art. 17.292(a) offense, the arresting/custodial person must give the magistrate the information needed to issue a magistrate's order for emergency protection.
  • (b) — for a misdemeanor punishable by fine only, once the magistrate has certainly identified the accused, the magistrate may release without bond and order a later arraignment appearance (written order stating time/date/place). No-show triggers a warrant, and if rearrested, bail is generally set at double the usual amount. This track doesn't apply to someone with a prior felony/non-fine-only-misdemeanor conviction, or a civilly committed sexually violent predator.
  • (c) — a deaf accused gets a qualified, sworn interpreter (per Art. 38.31) for the warnings.
  • (d) — for an Art. 14.051 arrest, if the magistrate finds the arrest was unlawful, the person is released; if lawful, they're treated as a fugitive from justice under Art. 51.13.
  • (e)–(f) — a record must specifically capture that the person was told of the right to request counsel, was asked whether they want to, and what they answered; the record format follows the county's Art. 26.04(a) procedures.
  • (g) — someone who appears on a misdemeanor citation under Art. 14.06(b)/(c) gets the same Art. 15.17 treatment as if arrested and brought in, and is released on personal bond absent good cause; failing to appear on an Art. 14.06(c) citation triggers a warrant.
  • (h) — if the magistrate finds no probable cause, written findings supporting that must be entered into the record within 24 hours.
Why it matters on patrol: the 48-hour clock starts at the moment of arrest, not when paperwork is finished or the officer's shift ends — and it's the arresting/custodial officer's duty (not just the magistrate's) to get the person there in time. Missing the window doesn't void the arrest, but it can affect probable-cause and bail litigation down the line, and repeated delay patterns draw scrutiny fast.
Art. 15.18–.21 ARREST FOR OUT-OF-COUNTY OFFENSE; NOTICE OF ARREST; DUTY OF SHERIFF RECEIVING NOTICE; RELEASE IF NOT TIMELY DEMANDED ▾

Art. 15.18 – ARREST FOR OUT-OF-COUNTY OFFENSE

What the local magistrate does with someone arrested on another county's warrant.

Someone arrested on a warrant issued in a different county is taken before a magistrate of the arrest county (or, to deliver the Art. 15.17 warnings faster, before any other county's magistrate, including the issuing county's). That magistrate either takes bail (if allowed) and, if without jurisdiction, immediately transmits the bond to the court that has jurisdiction — or, for a fine-only warrant offense, may accept a written guilty/nolo plea, set the fine, determine costs, accept payment, credit time served, determine indigency, and discharge the defendant once satisfied.

If magistrated outside the issuing county, the arrestee must still be told the counsel-appointment procedures, with request forms transmitted to the issuing county's appointing authority within 24 hours of a request. A magistrate who accepted a guilty/nolo plea without jurisdiction must transmit the plea, orders, and any collected fine/costs to the court with jurisdiction within 11 business days. Appearance may be by electronic broadcast system per Art. 15.17. This article doesn't apply to an arrest on a capias pro fine (Chapter 43 or Art. 45A.259).

Art. 15.19 – NOTICE OF ARREST

What happens if the out-of-county arrestee can't or won't make bail: jail, plus a notice to the home county.

If the arrestee fails or refuses to give bail, they're committed to the jail of the arrest county, and the committing magistrate must immediately notify the sheriff of the county where the offense allegedly happened — of the arrest/commitment, and of whether the person was also arrested on a Government Code §508.251 (parole-violator) warrant. If magistrated in a county other than where arrested, the person may be held in the magistrate's county jail up to 72 hours before transfer to the arrest county's jail.

Art. 15.20 – DUTY OF SHERIFF RECEIVING NOTICE

The sheriff who receives that notice must forthwith go or send for the arrestee and bring them before the proper court or magistrate — or, for a §508.251 parole-warrant notice, before the 11th day after commitment.

Art. 15.21 – RELEASE ON PERSONAL BOND IF NOT TIMELY DEMANDED

The backstop: if nobody comes to get the arrestee, they don't sit in jail forever.

If the proper office of the offense county hasn't demanded and taken charge of the arrestee before the 11th day after commitment, a magistrate in the arrest county must release the person on personal bond without sureties, and forward that bond to the offense-county sheriff or the issuing court.

Art. 15.22–.26 WHEN A PERSON IS ARRESTED; TIME OF ARREST; FORCE; BREAKING A DOOR; AUTHORITY MUST BE MADE KNOWN ▾

Art. 15.22 – WHEN A PERSON IS ARRESTED

The moment-of-arrest definition, warrant or no warrant.

A person is arrested when actually placed under restraint or taken into custody — whether by an officer/person executing a warrant, or by one arresting without a warrant.

Art. 15.23 – TIME OF ARREST

An arrest may be made on any day, at any time of the day or night.

Art. 15.24 – WHAT FORCE MAY BE USED

The general force standard for effecting an arrest: reasonable means, no more than necessary.

All reasonable means are permitted to effect an arrest — but no greater force may be used than is necessary to secure the arrest and detention of the accused.

Art. 15.25 – MAY BREAK DOOR

Texas's version of "knock and announce" — felony arrests only.

In a felony case, the officer may break down the door of any house to make the arrest, if refused admittance after giving notice of authority and purpose.

Cross-reference: this is the "no-knock" answer for Chapter 18. Texas doesn't have a separate "no-knock warrant" category for search warrants either — the closest statutory knock-and-announce text in the whole Code is this article, and it's an arrest provision, not a search-warrant one. See Chapter 18's note on this. Notice-then-refusal is the trigger; there's no forced-entry authority for a misdemeanor arrest under this article.

Art. 15.26 – AUTHORITY TO ARREST MUST BE MADE KNOWN

The officer doesn't need the warrant in hand — but the arrestee must always know why they're being arrested, and the warrant itself is public record.

Executing a warrant, the accused must always be told under what authority the arrest is made. The warrant is executed by the arrest itself. The officer doesn't need to have the warrant physically in hand at the time of arrest (as long as it was validly issued under this Code) — but must show it to the defendant on request as soon as possible. If the officer doesn't have it, the officer must instead tell the defendant the offense charged and that a warrant has issued.

Public record duty: the warrant and any supporting affidavit are public information the moment the warrant is executed — the magistrate's clerk must make copies available for public inspection during normal business hours, and provide copies on payment of the reproduction cost.
Art. 15.27 NOTIFICATION TO SCHOOLS REQUIRED ▾

Art. 15.27 – NOTIFICATION TO SCHOOLS REQUIRED

A long, detail-heavy article, but the core pattern is simple: arrest a student for a qualifying offense, and the school has to know.

When a law enforcement agency arrests (or juvenile-refers) someone it believes is enrolled in a public or private primary/secondary school, for an offense listed below, the agency must try to determine enrollment. If the person is (or is believed to be) enrolled, the agency head or a designee must orally notify the superintendent (or designee) within 24 hours of the arrest/referral, or before the next school day — whichever is earlier. The superintendent then immediately notifies instructional/support personnel with supervisory responsibility for the student, and everyone who receives the information must keep it confidential. A written follow-up, marked "PERSONAL and CONFIDENTIAL," must follow within 7 days, repeating the oral notice's facts.

The same two-step (oral, then written) notification duty applies to the prosecutor's office on a conviction, deferred prosecution, deferred adjudication, or delinquent-conduct adjudication — including whether the student must register as a sex offender under Chapter 62. Parole/probation/community-supervision offices have a parallel duty when a covered student transfers schools or districts. Private schools get the same notifications, routed to the principal instead of a superintendent.

Qualifying offenses (subsection (h)): any felony, plus these misdemeanors — PC §20.02 (unlawful restraint), §21.08 (indecent exposure), §22.01 (assault), §22.05 (deadly conduct), §22.07 (terroristic threat), §71.02 (engaging in organized criminal activity); unlawful use/sale/possession of a controlled substance, drug paraphernalia, or marihuana; and unlawful possession of the weapons/devices in PC §46.01(1)–(14)/(16) or a prohibited weapon under §46.05.

Electronic notification can substitute for oral notification (and then no separate written notice is required). Unauthorized disclosure of information received under this article is a Class C misdemeanor. Failures to notify get reported up the chain — to the State Board for Educator Certification (school-side failures), to TCOLE (law-enforcement-side failures), or to the elected prosecutor (prosecutor-office failures).

Why it matters on patrol: this duty sits with the arresting agency, not the school — if you arrest someone you believe is a student for a qualifying offense, the 24-hour notification clock is running on your agency the moment the arrest happens, separately from anything the magistration process under Art. 15.17 is doing.
Art. 15.171 DUTY OF OFFICER TO NOTIFY PROBATE COURT ▾

Art. 15.171 – DUTY OF OFFICER TO NOTIFY PROBATE COURT

A narrow, easy-to-miss notice duty: arresting someone under a legal guardianship.

"Ward" has the meaning assigned by Estates Code §22.033 (a person for whom a guardian has been appointed). As soon as practicable — but no later than the first working day after the arrest — the arresting officer or the person with custody of the ward must notify the court with jurisdiction over that ward's guardianship of the arrest.

Practical note: this duty is easy to overlook because it isn't part of the standard booking/magistration paperwork under Art. 15.17 — it depends on the officer recognizing (or being told) that the arrestee has an active guardianship, which usually only surfaces if it comes up during the arrest or booking interview.
