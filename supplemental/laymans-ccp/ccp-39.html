Art. 39.14
Art. 39.14 DISCOVERY (THE "MICHAEL MORTON ACT") ▾

Art. 39.14 – DISCOVERY

The state's discovery-disclosure obligation to the defense — and the statute Art. 2A.209's agency certification duty exists to enforce.

On a timely request from the defendant, the State must produce — and let the defense inspect or electronically copy, as soon as practicable — offense reports, witness statements (including officers' own statements), documents, photographs, physical objects, and other tangible items in the State's possession, custody, or control (prosecution work product is excluded). Texas courts read "the State" broadly here: it includes material held by the investigating law-enforcement agency, not just what's physically sitting in the prosecutor's file.

  • Expert witnesses (subsection (b)): on a request made 30+ days before trial, the State must disclose each expert's name, address, and qualifications no later than 20 days before trial.
  • Mandatory exculpatory/impeachment/mitigating disclosure (subsection (h)): the State must turn over any document, item, or information that tends to negate guilt or reduce punishment — automatically, whether or not the defense asked for it. This is the codification of the Brady/Giglio duty.
  • Jailhouse informants (subsection (h-1)): if the State plans to use testimony from someone who was incarcerated with the defendant, it must disclose that informant's full criminal history, any leniency deal, and other cases where they've testified against a fellow inmate.
  • Continuing duty (subsection (k)): exculpatory, impeachment, or mitigating material discovered after the initial disclosure — even mid-trial or post-trial — must be disclosed promptly, not held for the next case.
Why this matters for an officer: a report, note, photo, or piece of evidence sitting in an agency's file — even something the prosecutor never asked for or didn't know existed — is still discoverable material under current case law (State v. Heath, Tex. Crim. App. 2024; Moten v. State, Tex. Crim. App. 2025). "The prosecutor didn't have it" is not a defense to a disclosure failure if the agency had it. That's exactly the gap Art. 2A.209's certification requirement is designed to close — see Ch. 2A, Art. 2A.209.
