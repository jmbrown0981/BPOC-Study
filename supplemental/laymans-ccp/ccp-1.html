Art. 1.01 SHORT TITLE ▾

Art. 1.01 – SHORT TITLE

This is the article that names the code — "the Code of Criminal Procedure."

A one-line article: the Act that governs criminal procedure in Texas is officially cited as the "Code of Criminal Procedure" (CCP). Every citation you see throughout this guide — "CCP Art. X.XX" — traces back to this naming article.

Art. 1.03 OBJECTS OF THIS CODE ▾

Art. 1.03 – OBJECTS OF THIS CODE

The CCP's mission statement — why the code exists and what it's trying to accomplish.

The Code of Criminal Procedure exists to make the rules for preventing and prosecuting crime clear — both to the officers who work under them and to the people whose rights those rules affect. Article 1.03 lists six goals the Code seeks:

  • Prevent crime — adopt measures to stop offenses before they happen.
  • Deny escape — exclude offenders from any hope of avoiding accountability.
  • Speedy trial — ensure a trial happens with as little delay as is consistent with justice.
  • Full investigation — bring all evidence bearing on conviction or acquittal to the trial.
  • Fair trial — insure the trial itself is fair and impartial.
  • Certain execution — carry out the sentence the law prescribes once it's declared.
Why it matters: this article is the lens for interpreting everything else in the Code — when a specific procedural rule is ambiguous, these six objects are what courts fall back on to explain the rule's purpose.
Art. 1.04–.06 DUE COURSE OF LAW, RIGHTS OF ACCUSED & SEARCHES AND SEIZURES ▾

Art. 1.04 – DUE COURSE OF LAW

The CCP's own version of due process — nobody loses life, liberty, or property except through the law's own procedures.

No citizen may be deprived of life, liberty, property, privileges, or immunities — or in any manner disfranchised — except by due course of the law of the land.

Art. 1.05 – RIGHTS OF ACCUSED

The trial-rights package: speedy public trial, notice of the charge, silence, confrontation, compulsory process, and — for felonies — grand jury indictment.

In all criminal prosecutions, the accused has the right to a speedy public trial by an impartial jury; to demand and receive a copy of the accusation; to not be compelled to give evidence against themselves; to be heard in person, by counsel, or both; to be confronted with the witnesses against them; and to compulsory process for obtaining favorable witnesses. No person may be held to answer for a felony except on grand jury indictment.

Art. 1.06 – SEARCHES AND SEIZURES

The CCP's own Fourth Amendment analog — no warrant without a particular description and sworn probable cause.

People are secure in their persons, houses, papers, and possessions from unreasonable seizures or searches. No warrant to search a place or seize a person or thing may issue without describing them as near as possible, and without probable cause supported by oath or affirmation.

Art. 1.07–.09 RIGHT TO BAIL, HABEAS CORPUS & CRUELTY FORBIDDEN ▾

Art. 1.07 – RIGHT TO BAIL

Bail is the default — denial is the exception, and it has to be expressly authorized.

Any person is eligible for bail unless denial of bail is expressly permitted by the Texas Constitution or other law. This doesn't prevent setting bail after indictment based on an examination of the evidence, in whatever manner the law prescribes.

Ties to Chapter 17: the "expressly permitted" denial exceptions are exactly what Ch. 17 (Art. 17.152, 17.153) and the November 2025 Prop 3 constitutional amendment spell out — this article is the general rule those provisions carve exceptions into.

Art. 1.08 – HABEAS CORPUS

The writ of habeas corpus is a writ of right and may never be suspended.

Art. 1.09 – CRUELTY FORBIDDEN

Excessive bail may not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel or unusual punishment inflicted.

Art. 1.10–.11 JEOPARDY & ACQUITTAL A BAR ▾

Art. 1.10 – JEOPARDY

Double jeopardy, restated in the CCP.

No person may be twice put in jeopardy of life or liberty for the same offense, nor tried again for the same offense after a not-guilty verdict in a court of competent jurisdiction.

Art. 1.11 – ACQUITTAL A BAR

An acquittal sticks — with one narrow exception.

An acquittal bars a second trial or prosecution for the same offense, however irregular the original proceedings were — unless the acquittal happened in a court that had no jurisdiction over the offense, in which case the person can be prosecuted again in a court that does.

Art. 1.12–.15 RIGHT TO JURY, WAIVER OF JURY, WAIVER OF RIGHTS & JURY IN FELONY ▾

Art. 1.12 – RIGHT TO JURY

The right of trial by jury remains inviolate.

Art. 1.13 – WAIVER OF TRIAL BY JURY

A defendant can give up the jury right — but the waiver has strict formalities, especially in death-penalty cases.

A defendant in a non-capital case (or a capital case where the state won't seek death) may waive a jury trial, but the waiver must be made in person, in writing, in open court, with the consent and approval of both the court and the state's attorney. In a capital case where the state will seek death, jury waiver isn't available. A defendant may waive regardless of whether they have an attorney at the time — but if the defendant is charged with a felony and has no attorney, the court must appoint one before the waiver can happen.

Art. 1.14 – WAIVER OF RIGHTS

Most rights are waivable — including, critically, objections to a defective charging instrument if you don't raise them in time.

A defendant may waive most statutory rights (a capital defendant's jury-waiver option is limited to Art. 1.13(b)'s terms). If a defendant doesn't object to a defect, error, or irregularity in an indictment or information before trial on the merits begins, that objection is forfeited — it can't be raised later, including on appeal.

Art. 1.15 – JURY IN FELONY

No felony conviction without a jury verdict — unless the defendant validly waived it and the state still has to prove its case.

No one can be convicted of a felony except on a jury's verdict, unless the defendant validly waived the jury right under Art. 1.13/1.14. Even then, the state must introduce evidence into the record showing guilt — a defendant can never be convicted on a plea alone without sufficient supporting evidence, though that evidence can be stipulated if the defendant consents in writing to waive live witness testimony.

Art. 1.16–.19 LIBERTY OF SPEECH AND PRESS, RELIGIOUS BELIEF, OUTLAWRY & CORRUPTION OF BLOOD ▾

Art. 1.16 – LIBERTY OF SPEECH AND PRESS

Every person may speak, write, or publish their opinion on any subject (being liable for abuse of that privilege), and no law may curtail speech or press. In libel prosecutions involving publications on the conduct of public officers, or matters of public interest, the truth may be given in evidence, and the jury decides both law and fact.

Art. 1.17 – RELIGIOUS BELIEF

No person is disqualified from giving evidence in court based on religious opinions or lack of religious belief — oaths/affirmations are administered in whatever mode is most binding on that person's conscience, subject to perjury penalties either way.

Art. 1.18 – OUTLAWRY AND TRANSPORTATION

No citizen may be outlawed, and no person may be transported out of the state for an offense committed within it.

Art. 1.19 – CORRUPTION OF BLOOD, ETC.

No conviction works "corruption of blood" or forfeiture of estate — a conviction doesn't taint a defendant's heirs or strip inheritance rights the way it could under old English common law.

Art. 1.21 PRIVILEGE OF LEGISLATORS ▾

Art. 1.21 – PRIVILEGE OF LEGISLATORS

A narrow arrest immunity for sitting legislators — and it doesn't cover the offenses that matter most on patrol.

State Senators and Representatives are privileged from arrest during a legislative session, and while traveling to or from it (one day allowed per twenty miles from the Capitol) — except in cases of treason, felony, or breach of the peace. In practice, that carve-out swallows most of what an officer would actually be arresting someone for.

Art. 1.24–.25 PUBLIC TRIAL & CONFRONTED BY WITNESSES ▾

Art. 1.24 – PUBLIC TRIAL

Proceedings and trials in all courts are public.

Art. 1.25 – CONFRONTED BY WITNESSES

At trial, the defendant must be confronted with the witnesses against them, except where this Code allows depositions to substitute.

Art. 1.051 RIGHT TO REPRESENTATION BY COUNSEL ▾

Art. 1.051 – RIGHT TO REPRESENTATION BY COUNSEL

The right-to-counsel article that sets the actual appointment clock — this is what Art. 15.17 magistration duties plug into.

A defendant is entitled to counsel in an adversarial judicial proceeding, including the right to consult privately with counsel far enough in advance to prepare. An indigent defendant is entitled to appointed counsel in any proceeding that could result in confinement, or any other proceeding where the interests of justice require it.

  • Appointment clock: once an indigent defendant requests counsel and adversarial proceedings have started, the court (or its designee) must appoint counsel by the end of the 3rd working day after the request — or the 1st working day in a county of 250,000+ population.
  • Out-of-county warrants: if arrested on another county's warrant, the issuing county's appointment clock runs the same way — but if the defendant hasn't been transferred there within 10 days and no counsel's been appointed, the arresting county must immediately appoint counsel for any Chapter 11/17 matter (bail/habeas issues), regardless of whether the issuing county has started proceedings.
  • State may not pressure a waiver: the prosecutor may not initiate or encourage an unrepresented defendant to waive counsel, or communicate with a defendant who's requested appointed counsel, until that request has been resolved and the defendant's had a real opportunity to retain private counsel.
  • Waiver must be knowing and voluntary, in writing, using the statutory form language — and can be withdrawn at any time (though withdrawing doesn't entitle the defendant to redo a proceeding already held).
Why it matters on patrol: this article is the backbone of the magistration process every arrestee goes through under Art. 15.17 — the appointment-request forms, timelines, and state-contact restrictions all trace back here.
Art. 1.141 WAIVER OF INDICTMENT FOR NONCAPITAL FELONY ▾

Art. 1.141 – WAIVER OF INDICTMENT FOR NONCAPITAL FELONY

A represented defendant can skip the grand jury for anything short of a capital case.

A person represented by legal counsel may voluntarily waive, in open court or in writing, the right to be accused by grand jury indictment — for any offense other than a capital felony. Once waived, the accused is instead charged by information.
