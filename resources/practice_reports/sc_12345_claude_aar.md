1. Legal basis for what you did

The offense: Criminal Trespass, PC 30.05.

(a) A person commits the offense if they enter or remain in a building or property of another, without effective consent, and (1) had notice that entry was forbidden.
(b)(2)(A) Notice can be oral or written communication “by the owner or someone with apparent authority to act for the owner.” That’s how the prior CTW gets you the notice element. It only works if the CTW was given on the owner’s behalf, for this property, and he received it.
(d)(3)(A)(i) The offense is a Class A misdemeanor if committed in a habitation. PC 30.01(1) defines a habitation as a structure “adapted for the overnight accommodation of persons.” A furnished rental house qualifies, so this was a Class A, not the default Class B. Your report doesn’t state the class.

Supporting definitions:

“Effective consent,” PC 1.07(a)(19): the owner, or someone legally authorized to act for the owner, has to give it.
“Owner,” PC 1.07(a)(35): the person with title, or a greater right to possession than the actor. The caller had title.

Burglary, PC 30.02, probably doesn’t fit. It requires entering a habitation with intent to commit a felony, theft or assault, or actually committing one. “I use it occasionally to sleep” shows no such intent, and nothing was reported missing. If the owner later finds something gone, that changes: burglary of a habitation is a second-degree felony.

The arrest: CCP 14.01(b). A peace officer may arrest without a warrant for any offense committed in their presence or within their view. Trespass by “remaining” is a continuing offense that was happening in front of you, so no warrant was needed. That only holds if you had probable cause on every element, and that’s where the notice question becomes critical (see section 3).

The entry: consent. You had no warrant and no emergency, so the entry rests entirely on the owner’s consent.

The project’s case list doesn’t include any consent cases. The standard ones, which you should confirm with your instructors, are:
Schneckloth v. Bustamonte (1973): consent must be voluntary.
United States v. Matlock (1974): a person with common authority over a place can consent to its search.
Illinois v. Rodriguez (1990): entry is still valid if officers reasonably believed the person consenting had authority, even if they turn out not to.
Payton v. New York (in the project) is what the defense will raise: police generally can’t enter a person’s home to arrest them without a warrant or an emergency. Your answer is that this wasn’t his home. He had no lease and no permission, and the owner, who holds title, consented. That answer only works if the report establishes those facts.

Standing: someone trespassing generally has no reasonable expectation of privacy in the place, so he can’t challenge the entry. That rule comes from Rakas v. Illinois (1978) and later cases, also not in the project. It falls apart if he had any colorable claim to be there. He told you he did, and the report doesn’t follow up.

Detention and handcuffing before the arrest. Before you announced the arrest, he was being detained to investigate, under Terry v. Ohio. Handcuffing during a detention is allowed when officer-safety facts justify it, but the report says only “for scene safety,” which is a conclusion. You didn’t frisk him when you cuffed him, and the knife in his shoe was only found later in the search after arrest.

Drawn firearms. Clearing a house for a possible unknown intruder supports having weapons out, judged from a reasonable officer’s view at that moment (Graham v. Connor). Once he was seated, compliant and had his hands up, the reasonableness of continuing to point weapons at him gets scrutinized. Robinson v. Solano County held that pointing a gun at the head of a compliant, non-threatening person at close range can be excessive force. The report never says when the firearms were holstered.

Searching him: search incident to arrest. This is valid if the arrest was lawful (Chimel v. California covers the person and the area within reach). The knife and the bag are only admissible if the trespass arrest stands. CCP 38.23 is the Texas rule that throws out evidence obtained in violation of the law.

Searching the house: the weakest legal piece.

A search incident to arrest (Chimel) doesn’t cover the whole house.
A protective sweep (Maryland v. Buie) is limited to looking for people who could be a danger. Your report says the purpose was to look for “missing items… other substances or weapons,” which is an evidence search.
The only thing that could support it is the owner’s consent. But the report only says he consented to “enter the residence to search,” which reads like searching for a person.

Nothing was found, so there’s nothing to suppress this time. Next time, the owner should do the walk-through for missing property, or the report should state the scope of his consent.

**The knife:** A 3-inch blade is not a location-restricted knife. Those have a blade over 5½ inches (PC 46.01(6)), so carrying it isn’t an offense. It’s a deadly weapon only if it was designed or adapted to kill or cause serious injury, or if he used or intended to use it that way (PC 1.07(a)(17)). If it qualifies, PC 30.05(d)(3)(C) makes the trespass a Class A anyway, so it adds nothing to the charge. It’s relevant to officer safety, not to the case.

**The powder:** If it’s a Penalty Group 1 substance such as cocaine or methamphetamine, 4 oz (about 113 g) falls in the 4 g to under 200 g range of HSC 481.115(d). That’s a second-degree felony, weighed “by aggregate weight, including adulterants or dilutants” (bulking material mixed in counts toward the weight). But nothing in the report identifies the substance, says how it was weighed, or charges it. As written, you arrested for trespass only and found a bag of unidentified powder.

**His statements:** “I use it occasionally to sleep” came at gunpoint, with his hands up, before any warnings.

A defense attorney will argue that was custodial interrogation under Miranda v. Arizona and CCP 38.22. An oral statement from custodial interrogation is admissible only if it’s recorded and preceded by the warnings (38.22 §3).
“I’m here legally” sounds volunteered, which 38.22 §5 doesn’t bar.
His name and date of birth are routine booking-type questions, and those are fine.

**How a defense attorney would take you apart:**

“Who gave consent?” Your report doesn’t name the person who authorized the entry, or show he had authority. Without that, the entry was warrantless, and the defense moves to suppress everything after the door opened: the arrest, the knife, the powder and the statements (CCP 38.23).

“My client lived there.” He said he sleeps there and was there legally, and you didn’t investigate. The defense says this was a landlord–tenant dispute, so the owner’s consent didn’t cover his space, Payton applies, and it’s a civil matter.

“What notice?” Notice is an element of trespass. The report never says the CTW covered this address, was served on him, or was still valid. Without notice, there was no probable cause to arrest, and the search after arrest falls with it.

“Unwarned statement at gunpoint.” Guns drawn, hands up, then “what are you doing here?” The defense moves to suppress the statement. They’ll also note that he was handcuffed before you knew who he was, with “scene safety” as the only stated reason.

“Excessive force.” Firearms pointed at a seated man, possibly a wheelchair user, who complied with every command, and the report never says when you holstered.

“A bag of what?” The substance is unidentified, untested and weighed “approximately,” with no word on whether the bag was included. There’s no evidence number and no chain of custody, and it wasn’t even charged.

“The house search was a fishing expedition.” By your own words it was a search for evidence, beyond both Chimel and Buie.

“Your report contradicts itself.” Officer Mary tells him he’s under arrest twice, once before the search and once at the end, so which is it? 

And then there are the typos: “inteviewing,” “entrace,” “residince,” “powedery,” “appromiately.” Each one chips at your credibility on the stand.

“How do you know he’s John Brown?” You took his name and date of birth from him and “called it in.” Was there a photo ID, or a photo match from the prior report?
