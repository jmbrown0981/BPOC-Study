Smuggling vs. Trafficking

21.2 / 21.8 Consent, Exploitation & Transnationality — the Three-Part Test ▾

BPOC draws the line using three factors. Consent: the smuggled person agrees to being moved; a trafficking victim either never agreed to be moved, or agreed only because they were deceived by false promises. Exploitation: smuggling ends at the destination when the smuggler and smuggled person part ways; trafficking continues exploiting the victim at the destination and/or during the journey itself. Transnationality: smuggling always requires crossing an international border; trafficking occurs regardless of whether any border was crossed, or whether the victim moved at all.

The critical legal point: per the Palermo Protocol and U.S./Texas law, a person's initial consent — even consent to be smuggled illegally across a border — becomes irrelevant the instant they're forced into labor or exploited against their will at the destination. Any smuggled migrant becomes a trafficking victim the moment that happens, regardless of what they agreed to at the outset.

21.10 The Mens Rea Continuum — Reading Victim Knowledge as an Indicator ▾

Webb & Myers frame this as a continuum of the victim's own knowledge/awareness of what travel would actually involve — a useful investigative lens, not a legal test on its own:

Full knowledge, no demandsCollusion with complete awareness of the outcome, and no demands placed on the person afterward — points toward smuggling, not trafficking.
Some knowledge, demands attachedSome awareness of the outcome, plus demands (e.g., work in the entertainment industry) — not necessarily trafficking unless the work is induced by force, ID documents are confiscated, or the person is denied freedom.
No knowledge of outcome or demandsNo awareness at all of what travel would involve or what would be demanded — a clear trafficking indicator, and a likely sign of organized criminal activity.

Prostitution vs. Trafficking

21.2 “Being Prostituted Out” Is Not an Accurate Description of Trafficking ▾

Law enforcement has historically described a trafficking victim as someone “being prostituted out to clients” — BPOC calls this incorrect. A person engaged in prostitution can be a trafficking victim, but a trafficking victim should never be labeled a “prostitute.” A sex-trafficking victim is legally and functionally a sexual assault victim, not someone voluntarily selling sex — even where a commercial-sex offense is technically what a bystander or officer observes.

For a minor, this isn't even a close call: because a child cannot legally consent, child sex trafficking and Commercial Sexual Exploitation of Children (CSEC) require no proof of force, fraud, or coercion at all — unlike adult trafficking, where those three elements must be shown.

Where They Overlap

OVERLAP The Escalation Chain & the Statutory Cross-References ▾

Prostitution → Compelling Prostitution → Trafficking: PC §20A.02(a)(3)-(4) makes it trafficking to traffic another person and, through force/fraud/coercion, cause them to engage in conduct prohibited by the prostitution-family offenses — PC §43.02 (Prostitution), §43.03/.031 (Promotion), §43.04/.041 (Aggravated Promotion), or §43.05 (Compelling Prostitution) — or to receive a benefit from a venture involving that activity. In other words: the same underlying conduct can be charged as prostitution, as compelling prostitution, or folded into a trafficking charge, depending on how the person came to be doing it and who benefits.

The affirmative defense runs the other way, too: PC §43.02(d) gives a person charged with prostitution an affirmative defense if they engaged in that conduct because they were themselves a victim of trafficking (§20A.02) or compelling prostitution (§43.05). This is the statutory backbone of BPOC's instruction not to simply arrest a suspected trafficking victim on a prostitution charge.

Smuggling → Trafficking: BPOC Ch.21.11 notes that trafficker roles are “sometimes assigned to human smugglers who are not looking to traffic anyone, yet the smuggling turns into trafficking once a person is in the United States” — the conversion described in the Smuggling vs. Trafficking section above.

Victim protections that follow from the trafficking label: Family Code §261.001(1)(G) & (L) write trafficking (PC §20A.02) directly into the definition of child “abuse.” And CCP Art. 56A.052(e) gives a victim convicted of (or placed on deferred adjudication for) an offense committed solely as a result of being trafficked — prostitution being the textbook example — the right to be informed they may petition for nondisclosure of that criminal history record.

Statute & Punishment Reference

PC 20.05 Smuggling of Persons ▾

Elements — a person commits an offense if they knowingly: (1) use a motor vehicle, aircraft, or watercraft to transport an individual with intent to conceal that individual from a peace officer, or to flee from a person the actor knows is a peace officer attempting to arrest/detain the actor; (2) encourage or induce a person to enter or remain in the U.S. in violation of federal law, by concealing, harboring, or shielding that person; or (3) assist two or more individuals to enter or remain on agricultural land without the effective consent of the owner.

Punishment — PC §20.05(b)
Felony, 3rd DegreeBase offenseenhanced min. ~10 yrs
Felony, 2nd DegreeSBI/death substantial likelihood; victim <18; pecuniary benefit; firearm; or fleeing a peace officerenhanced min. ~10 yrs
Felony, 1st DegreeVictim suffers sexual assault, or serious bodily injury/death resultsheightened minimum — verify exact figure against current statute

Mitigation: the base 3rd-degree minimum can drop to 5 years if the state certifies the defendant gave “significant cooperation” to law enforcement, or if the actor is related to the smuggled person within the third degree of consanguinity/affinity. A relationship within the second degree of consanguinity/affinity is a full affirmative defense (with exceptions for the more serious enhanced scenarios).

PC 20.06 Continuous Smuggling of Persons ▾

Elements: engaging two or more times in conduct that constitutes an offense under §20.05, over a period of 10 or more days. As with continuous trafficking (PC §20A.03), a jury doesn't need to unanimously agree on the specific act or exact date — only that the pattern of two-or-more instances occurred within that 10+ day window. A defendant generally can't be convicted of both a §20.05 offense and §20.06 for the same victim, except where the §20.05 charge is pled in the alternative, falls outside the alleged continuous-smuggling period, or is a lesser-included offense.

Punishment — PC §20.06
Felony, 2nd DegreeBase offenseenhanced min. ~10 yrs
Felony, 1st DegreeSubstantial risk of SBI/death, or victim <18enhanced min. ~10 yrs
Felony, 1st DegreeSexual assault, aggravated sexual assault, SBI, or death actually resulted25–99 yrs or life
PC 43.02 Prostitution ▾

Elements: a person commits an offense if they knowingly offer to engage, agree to engage, or engage in sexual conduct for a fee, or knowingly solicit another in a public place to engage in sexual conduct for hire. Punishment escalates with prior convictions for this same offense (whether the sentence was imposed, probated, or resulted from deferred adjudication).

Punishment — PC §43.02
Class B Misd.1st offense
Class A Misd.1–2 prior convictions
State Jail Felony3+ prior convictions

Affirmative defense (§43.02(d)): it's a defense to prosecution that the actor engaged in the conduct because they were themselves a victim of trafficking (§20A.02) or compelling prostitution (§43.05) — see Overlap, above.

PC 43.05 Compelling Prostitution ▾

Elements — a person commits an offense if they knowingly: (1) cause another by force, threat, coercion, or fraud to commit prostitution; (2) cause a child younger than 18 to commit prostitution, by any means, regardless of whether the actor knows the child's age; or (3) cause a disabled individual to commit prostitution, by any means, regardless of whether the actor knows the individual is disabled. Coercion here is defined broadly — it includes withholding a victim's identifying documents and causing (or exploiting) intoxication or chemical dependency to impair resistance. Lack of the victim's mental capacity, or an incomplete act, is not a defense in the child/disabled-individual scenarios.

Punishment — PC §43.05
Felony, 1st DegreeFlat classification — no lesser tier, regardless of theory

§20A.02: knowingly trafficking another person intending forced labor/services; receiving a benefit from a venture involving forced labor/services while knowing it's forced (no direct trafficking act required); trafficking someone into a prostitution-family offense through force/fraud/coercion; or trafficking a child/disabled individual into forced labor or specified sexual offenses — with the age/disability-knowledge requirement expressly waived.
