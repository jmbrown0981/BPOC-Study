# Chapter 6: Racial Profiling - Question Bank

## LO 6.1 - Identify the three levels of encounters between peace officers and individuals.

### Q1 — Recall
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

Which level of encounter allows individuals to not identify and freely leave whenever they deem appropriate?

- [x] Consensual encounter
- [ ] Investigative detentions/"Terry Stops"
- [ ] Arrest
- [ ] FAFO stops

**Explanation:** A consensual encounter requires no legal justification at all — the individual is free to decline to answer questions or walk away at any time. It only becomes an investigative detention once a reasonable person would no longer feel free to leave.

---

### Q2 — Recall
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

Which level of encounter requires **reasonable suspicion**, in which a hunch or gut feeling DOES NOT qualify?

- [ ] Consensual encounter
- [x] Investigative detentions/"Terry Stops"
- [ ] Arrest
- [ ] FAFO stops

**Explanation:** An investigative detention (Terry stop) requires reasonable suspicion — specific, articulable facts that a person committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime. A hunch or gut feeling, without more, does not meet this standard.

---

### Q3 — Recall
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

Which level of encounter requires **probable cause** and would lead a reasonable person/officer to believe a crime has been committed?

- [ ] Consensual encounter
- [ ] Investigative detentions/"Terry Stops"
- [x] Arrest
- [ ] FAFO stops

**Explanation:** An arrest requires probable cause — facts or evidence that would lead a reasonable person to believe a crime has been, is being, or will be committed. It's the highest of the three standards, above reasonable suspicion and far above a consensual encounter's "no justification needed."

---

### Q4 — Concept
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

After a lawful traffic stop, which of the following is **NOT** one of BPOC's recognized independent justifications for searching the vehicle or its occupants?

- [ ] Consent
- [ ] Probable cause
- [ ] Inventory search of an impounded vehicle
- [x] The officer's discretion based on the neighborhood the stop occurred in

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.1 lists four justifications for searching after a stop: consent, search incident to arrest, probable cause, and an inventory search of an impounded vehicle. "The neighborhood" isn't a legal justification for anything — a search still needs one of the four recognized bases regardless of where the stop happens.

---

### Q5 — Recall
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

BPOC lists five recognized theories under which an officer may lawfully initiate a traffic stop. Which of the following is **NOT** one of them?

- [ ] Traffic violations
- [ ] "Pretext" stops
- [x] The racial or ethnic composition of the neighborhood
- [ ] Citizen calls

**Explanation:** The five theories BPOC lists are: traffic violations, pretext stops, community caretaking, citizen calls, and roadblocks/checkpoints. The composition of a neighborhood is never, by itself, a legitimate basis for a stop — using it that way is exactly what racial profiling law prohibits.

---

### Q6 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC 6.1

An officer detains a driver after a lawful traffic stop, requesting identification, asking the reason for being in the area, and asking the driver to explain conduct the officer found suspicious — all before deciding whether to seek consent for a pat-down or search. Which level of encounter does this describe?

- [ ] Consensual encounter
- [x] Investigative detention ("Terry stop")
- [ ] Arrest
- [ ] Community caretaking contact

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.1's "temporary detention options" — detaining someone for a reasonable time to account for their activity, making reasonable investigative inquiries, and seeking consent for a pat-down or search — describe an investigative detention built on reasonable suspicion, not a fully voluntary consensual encounter or a probable-cause arrest.

### Q7 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC Required Scenario, "Consent Search Decision"

Officer Davis stops a vehicle for speeding. The driver seems nervous, is breathing rapidly, and has shaking hands. The vehicle has air fresheners hanging from the mirror, and Officer Davis notices out-of-state plates. Based on these facts alone, does Officer Davis have probable cause or reasonable suspicion to search the vehicle?

- [ ] Yes — the combination of nervousness, air fresheners, and out-of-state plates is enough on its own
- [x] No — nervousness is extremely common during stops, air fresheners are legal, and out-of-state plates are perfectly legal; none of it is indicative of criminal activity
- [ ] Yes, but only after ordering the driver to exit the vehicle
- [ ] No, because traffic stops never permit vehicle searches

**Explanation:** BPOC's own analysis of this scenario is direct: none of these three facts, individually or together, supports probable cause or reasonable suspicion. The recommended action is to issue the citation and conclude the stop — and the built-in bias check is to ask whether Officer Davis would request consent to search from a nervous white driver with the exact same characteristics.

### Q8 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.1 · **Source:** BPOC Required Scenario, "Probable Cause Search"

Officer Hamdan stops a vehicle for an improper lane change. During the conversation, Officer Hamdan detects a strong odor of marijuana coming from the vehicle. The driver admits to smoking marijuana earlier but claims none is currently in the vehicle, and the driver's eyes are red and bloodshot. Does Officer Hamdan have probable cause to search the vehicle?

- [ ] No — an admission of past use is never enough by itself
- [x] Yes — the marijuana odor, the driver's admission, and the physical signs of impairment together establish probable cause under the totality of the circumstances
- [ ] No — only a canine alert can establish probable cause for an odor-based search
- [ ] Yes, but only because the driver committed a traffic violation

**Explanation:** BPOC's analysis treats this as a constitutional search regardless of the driver's demographics: the odor is an objective observation, the admission is direct evidence, and the red, bloodshot eyes are a physical sign consistent with impairment. Document each specific observation — that documentation is what makes the probable-cause finding solid.

## LO 6.2 - Identify the legislative requirements regarding racial profiling.

### Q9 — Fill-in-Blank
**LO:** 6.2 · **Source:** BPOC 6.2

The ______ amendment to the U.S. Constitution guarantees equal protection under law for all citizens.

- [ ] First
- [ ] Fourth
- [x] Fourteenth
- [ ] Twentieth

**Explanation:** The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause guarantees that laws — and how they're enforced — apply equally regardless of race, ethnicity, or national origin. It works alongside the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches and seizures as the constitutional foundation for racial profiling law.

**Explanation:** CCP Art. 2B.0051(4) defines racial profiling as "a law enforcement-initiated action based on an individual's race, ethnicity, or national origin rather than on the individual's behavior or on information identifying the individual as having engaged in criminal activity."

### Q19 — Recall

Which of the following must a peace officer report to their employing agency after a motor vehicle stop, under CCP Art. 2B.0054?

- [x] The detained driver's gender and race or ethnicity, plus the initial reason for the stop
- [ ] The detained driver's income level

Criminal history, income, and immigration status aren't part of this required report.

## LO 6.3 - Explain Supreme Court decisions and other court decisions regarding traffic stops and prohibited racial profiling.

### Q20 — Concept
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806 (1996)

In *Whren v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that a traffic stop is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment when:

- [ ] The officer's true motive for the stop was a legitimate one
- [x] There is probable cause to believe any traffic violation occurred, regardless of the officer's subjective motive for making the stop
- [ ] The officer can prove race played no role in the decision to stop the vehicle
- [ ] The stop lasts less than 10 minutes

**Explanation:** *Whren* held that an officer's subjective motive doesn't factor into the Fourth Amendment reasonableness analysis — only whether an actual, objective traffic violation existed. This is exactly why CCP Art. 2B.0054's requirement to record the specific "reason for the stop" matters so much: since *Whren* means courts won't second-guess motive, the objective, documented reason is the only real check against a stop being pretext for profiling.

---

### Q21 — Recall
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968)

*Terry v. Ohio* (1968) established that an officer may briefly detain a person and pat them down for weapons based on:

- [ ] Probable cause
- [x] Reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot and that the person may be armed and dangerous
- [ ] A hunch developed from the person's race or the neighborhood
- [ ] A request from dispatch, without more

**Explanation:** *Terry* created the reasonable-suspicion standard for a brief investigative stop, plus a separate, narrower authority — a weapons pat-down — that requires its own independent reasonable belief the person is armed. Neither piece can rest on race, ethnicity, or the character of a neighborhood.

---

### Q22 — Concept
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106 (1977); Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408 (1997)

During a lawful traffic stop, an officer orders both the driver and a passenger out of the vehicle as a routine safety precaution, without any additional suspicion about either person specifically. Is this constitutional?

- [ ] No — each occupant needs independent reasonable suspicion before being ordered out
- [ ] Yes, but only the driver may be ordered out without extra justification
- [x] Yes — Mimms and Wilson together allow an officer to order any occupant, driver or passenger, out of a lawfully stopped vehicle without additional justification
- [ ] No — only a supervisor can authorize removing a passenger from the vehicle

**Explanation:** *Pennsylvania v. Mimms* held ordering the driver out needs no extra justification once the stop itself is lawful; *Maryland v. Wilson* extended the same rule to passengers. Neither case turns on race or suspicion about the individual occupant — it's a blanket officer-safety rule tied to the stop itself.

---

### Q23 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Graham v. State, 119 Md. App. 444 (1998); Pryor v. State, 122 Md. App. 671 (1997); Ferris v. State, 355 Md. 356 (1999)

An officer completes a traffic stop, issues the citation, and returns the driver's license and registration. With nothing new having developed during the stop, the officer then asks the driver to wait an additional 20 minutes for a K-9 unit to arrive and sniff the vehicle. Under the reasoning of cases like *Graham v. State*, *Pryor v. State*, and *Ferris v. State*, is this extended wait constitutional?

- [ ] Yes, because the original stop was lawful, so anything that follows it is automatically lawful too
- [ ] Yes, because a K-9 sniff is never considered a search
- [x] No — once the purpose of the stop is fulfilled, continuing to detain the driver requires new, independent reasonable suspicion that developed during the stop
- [ ] No — K-9 units may never be used in connection with a traffic stop

**Explanation:** These cases all hold the same line: once a stop's purpose is satisfied (citation written, documents returned), continued detention is a new seizure that needs its own justification. Waiting on a K-9 unit — or having a completely separate, independently valid basis to stop the person in the first place — doesn't buy unlimited extra time; each basis for detention has its own time budget.

---

### Q24 — Recall
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** New York v. Belton, 453 U.S. 454 (1981)

*New York v. Belton* addressed an officer's authority to:

- [ ] Order a passenger out of a vehicle during a stop
- [x] Search the passenger compartment of a vehicle incident to the lawful custodial arrest of a recent occupant
- [ ] Use a K-9 unit during a routine traffic stop
- [ ] Stop a vehicle based solely on an anonymous tip

**Explanation:** *Belton* addressed the scope of a search incident to arrest for a vehicle's recent occupant. (Its original bright-line scope has since been narrowed by *Arizona v. Gant*, but for BPOC 6.3's purposes, the key point is that *Belton* is about search-incident-to-arrest authority, not stop justification or occupant control.)

---

### Q25 — Concept
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Brendlin v. California, 551 U.S. 249 (2007)

In *Brendlin v. California*, the Supreme Court held that when police stop a vehicle:

- [ ] Only the driver is legally "seized" by the stop
- [x] Both the driver and any passengers are "seized" for the duration of the stop, and a passenger may independently challenge the stop's legality
- [ ] Only a passenger who is later arrested has standing to challenge the stop
- [ ] Passengers automatically consent to being questioned by remaining in the vehicle

**Explanation:** *Brendlin* held a passenger is seized right alongside the driver, meaning a passenger has standing to argue the stop itself was unlawful — relevant any time evidence found against a passenger traces back to a stop that may have been racially motivated or otherwise unjustified.

---

### Q26 — Concept
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Virginia v. Moore, 553 U.S. 164 (2008)

*Virginia v. Moore* held that an arrest supported by probable cause satisfies the Fourth Amendment even when:

- [ ] The officer used excessive force during the arrest
- [x] The arrest violated a state law that directed the officer to issue a citation instead of making a custodial arrest for that offense
- [ ] The officer misrepresented the reason for the stop
- [ ] The suspect was a minor

**Explanation:** *Moore* held that state law doesn't define the scope of federal constitutional protection — a probable-cause arrest is still reasonable under the Fourth Amendment even if it violated a state cite-and-release statute. That's a real constitutional floor, but it doesn't make ignoring a department's own citation policy consequence-free administratively — internal discipline can still follow even where the Constitution alone would allow the arrest.

---

### Q27 — Recall
**LO:** 6.3 · **Source:** Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323 (2009)

*Arizona v. Johnson* held that during a lawful traffic stop, an officer may pat down a passenger for weapons if:

- [ ] The passenger asks the officer a lot of questions
- [x] The officer develops independent reasonable suspicion the passenger is armed and dangerous, and the frisk doesn't measurably extend how long the stop takes
- [ ] The passenger is a different race than the driver
- [ ] The stop occurs after dark

**Explanation:** *Johnson* allows questioning and, with independent reasonable suspicion, frisking a passenger even though the stop had nothing to do with them originally — but the officer's authority to prolong the stop to develop that suspicion is not unlimited, and race is never a component of the reasonable-suspicion analysis.

## LO 6.4 - Identify the legal liabilities of racial profiling.

### Q28 — Concept
**LO:** 6.4 · **Source:** BPOC 6.4

Does qualified immunity protect an officer from civil liability in a racial profiling case?

- [ ] Yes, always
- [x] No — qualified immunity does not apply for racial profiling cases
- [ ] Only if the officer acted in good faith
- [ ] Only for federal officers, not state or local officers

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.4 specifically calls out that the usual qualified-immunity shield — which normally protects officers unless they violate a clearly established constitutional right — does not apply to racial profiling claims. This is one of the reasons racial profiling carries meaningfully higher personal liability exposure than many other discretionary decisions an officer makes.

- [ ] 10 years

### Q31 — Definition
**LO:** 6.4 · **Source:** BPOC 6.4

A civil rights lawsuit against an officer for racial profiling, based on conduct committed "under color of state law," is typically brought under:

- [ ] 42 U.S.C. § 1981
- [x] 42 U.S.C. § 1983
- [ ] 18 U.S.C. § 242
- [ ] The Texas Tort Claims Act

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.4 identifies 42 U.S.C. § 1983 as the civil-liability vehicle, requiring conduct "under color of state law" that deprives someone of a constitutional right. 18 U.S.C. § 242 is a related but distinct federal statute — it's a *criminal* deprivation-of-rights charge brought by federal prosecutors, not a civil suit a private plaintiff files.

### Q32 — Concept
**LO:** 6.4 · **Source:** BPOC 6.4

A city's traffic-stop data shows a consistent pattern of racial disparities in enforcement across multiple officers over several years, and the department took no corrective action despite the pattern. Under what theory could the municipality itself — not just the individual officers — be held liable?

- [ ] Respondeat superior, which automatically makes a city liable for any officer's conduct
- [x] Municipal liability, where the constitutional violations resulted from an official policy or custom
- [ ] Sovereign immunity, which bars any municipal liability
- [ ] Only the Texas Attorney General has authority to sue a municipality for this

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.4 explains that municipalities — including law enforcement agencies — can be held liable when constitutional violations are shown to have resulted from an official policy or custom, not merely an isolated individual officer's bad decision. A documented, ignored pattern is exactly the kind of evidence that can establish "custom."

### Q34 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.4 · **Source:** BPOC Optional Scenario, "Force Documentation"

Officer Martinez conducts a traffic stop. The driver becomes argumentative and exits the vehicle despite being ordered to remain inside. Officer Williams uses physical control to complete the arrest after the driver resists, and the driver sustains minor injuries. To defend against a later claim that the stop or the force used was influenced by the driver's race, what should the required documentation include?

- [ ] Only a note that the driver "resisted"
- [x] The underlying traffic violation justifying the stop, the specific circumstances necessitating force, the type and degree of force used, the injuries sustained, and an objective justification for the force level
- [ ] Only the injuries the driver sustained
- [ ] Nothing extra — body-worn camera footage alone is sufficient documentation

**Explanation:** BPOC's required documentation for this scenario covers the full chain: why the stop happened, why force became necessary, exactly what force was used, what injuries resulted, and an objective justification tying it all together. That complete record — not just the video — is what shows the force decision was based on the driver's conduct, not demographics.

## LO 6.5 - Explain the impact of racial profiling.

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.5 states this almost verbatim: a pattern of higher search rates paired with lower hit rates for a particular group may suggest searches are being driven by something other than legitimate evidence of criminal activity — a disparity worth investigating, not dismissing.

### Q39 — Scenario
**LO:** 6.5 · **Source:** BPOC Optional Scenario, "Pattern Recognition"

A supervisor's monthly data review shows 45 total stops: 28 Hispanic drivers (62%) and 17 white drivers (38%). Of those, 18 of the 28 Hispanic drivers were searched (64%), versus 3 of the 17 white drivers (18%). Contraband was found in 4 of the 18 searches of Hispanic drivers (22%), versus 2 of the 3 searches of white drivers (67%). What does this pattern suggest, and what does BPOC recommend?

**Explanation:** BPOC's own analysis of this exact data set: disproportionate stop rates, much higher search rates, and a *lower* hit rate for Hispanic drivers than for white drivers together suggest potential bias in enforcement decisions — the textbook trigger for supervisor intervention, additional training, and closer monitoring under BPOC 6.5's framework.

## LO 6.6 - Identify forms of racial profiling.

### Q40 — Definition
**LO:** 6.6 · **Source:** BPOC 6.6

An officer who deliberately targets individuals for enforcement based on their race, ethnicity, or national origin is engaging in:

- [ ] Implicit bias
- [ ] Statistical discrimination
- [x] Conscious discrimination
- [ ] Procedural justice

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.6 defines conscious discrimination as deliberately targeting individuals based on race, ethnicity, or national origin — a knowing, intentional choice, unlike implicit bias.

---

### Q41 — Definition
**LO:** 6.6 · **Source:** BPOC 6.6

Unconscious attitudes and stereotypes that affect an officer's perceptions and decisions — even when the officer sincerely believes they're being neutral and objective — describe:

- [ ] Conscious discrimination
- [x] Implicit bias
- [ ] Statistical discrimination
- [ ] Procedural justice

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.6 defines implicit bias as unconscious attitudes and stereotypes affecting perception and decisions, noting officers with implicit bias are more likely to perceive ambiguous or nervous behavior as suspicious when exhibited by a person of color — even while genuinely believing their decisions are neutral.

---

### Q42 — Definition
**LO:** 6.6 · **Source:** BPOC 6.6

Relying on demographic generalizations about a group, rather than an individual's actual observed behavior, to make an enforcement decision describes:

- [ ] Implicit bias
- [ ] Conscious discrimination
- [x] Statistical discrimination
- [ ] Neutrality

**Explanation:** BPOC 6.6 defines statistical discrimination as relying on demographic generalizations rather than individual behavior — treating a group-level assumption as if it told you something reliable about the specific person in front of you.

---
