# Chapter 10: Arrest, Search, Seizure — Question Bank

## LO 10.1 — Terminology associated with arrest

### Q1 — Definition
**LO:** 10.1 · **Source:** Penal Code Sec. 2.01

Penal Code Sec. 2.01 ("Proof Beyond a Reasonable Doubt") establishes which principle?

- [x] All persons are presumed innocent, and no person may be convicted unless each element of the offense is proved beyond a reasonable doubt; arrest or indictment alone creates no inference of guilt
- [ ] A defendant must prove their own innocence beyond a reasonable doubt
- [ ] Probable cause alone is sufficient for conviction at trial
- [ ] The burden of proof shifts to the defendant once an arrest has been made

**Explanation:** "All persons are presumed to be innocent and no person may be convicted of an offense unless each element of the offense is proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The fact that he has been arrested, confined, or indicted for, or otherwise charged with, the offense gives rise to no inference of guilt at his trial."

### Q2 — Concept
**LO:** 10.1 · **Source:** BPOC 10.1

Why does the law recognize two separate definitions of probable cause — one for arrest and one for search?

- [x] Because different types of information are required to establish probable cause in each instance
- [ ] Because search probable cause requires a higher burden than arrest probable cause
- [ ] Because the two definitions are functionally identical and the distinction is purely historical
- [ ] Because only search probable cause requires a magistrate's involvement

**Explanation:** Probable cause to arrest concerns belief that a person has committed a crime; probable cause to search concerns belief that evidence or contraband will be found in a particular place or on a particular person. The facts needed to satisfy each are not the same.

- [ ] The officer has a strong personal hunch that a crime has occurred
- [ ] A confidential informant has made any uncorroborated accusation
- [ ] The suspect has a prior criminal record for a similar offense

**Explanation:** Brinegar v. United States, 338 U.S. 160 (1949) is the foundational case defining probable cause to arrest in these terms.

### Q4 — Definition
**LO:** 10.1 · **Source:** Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132 (1925)

Per Carroll v. United States, probable cause to search exists when:

- [x] The facts and circumstances within the officers' knowledge, and of which they had reasonably trustworthy information, are sufficient to warrant a person of reasonable caution in the belief that seizable property would be found in a particular place or on a particular person
- [ ] An officer merely suspects that contraband might be present somewhere in the general area
- [ ] A citizen complaint alone, without any corroboration, has been received
- [ ] The location searched has a general reputation for criminal activity

**Explanation:** Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132 (1925) establishes the "reasonably trustworthy information" standard for probable cause to search, distinct from — but structurally parallel to — the Brinegar standard for arrest.

### Q5 — Concept
**LO:** 10.1 · **Source:** Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89 (1964)

In Beck v. Ohio, the Supreme Court warned that if an officer's subjective good faith alone were the test for probable cause, what would result?

- [x] The protection of the Fourth Amendment would evaporate, leaving people secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects only in the discretion of the police
- [ ] Officers would be required to obtain a warrant for every arrest, with no exceptions

- [ ] Probable cause would become a purely objective, mathematically calculable standard

**Explanation:** Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89 (1964) rejected a purely subjective "good faith" standard for probable cause, insisting on an objective, reviewable standard so the Fourth Amendment isn't left to police discretion alone.

### Q6 — Definition

## LO 10.2 — Three classifications of officer-citizen interactions

### Q14 — Definition

- [ ] Any interaction in which the officer has reasonable suspicion of criminal activity
- [ ] An interaction that always requires the citizen's written consent form
- [ ] An interaction where the citizen is legally obligated to answer at least one question

**Explanation:** Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983) is the source case for the consensual encounter standard — the hallmark is that the citizen remains free to disengage at any time.

### Q15 — Definition

- [ ] A consensual conversation the citizen is free to end at any time

**Explanation:** Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) is the landmark case establishing the investigative "stop and frisk," requiring reasonable suspicion rather than the higher probable cause standard.

### Q19 — Concept
**LO:** 10.2 · **Source:** BPOC 10.2

What is the key legal distinction that separates the three classifications of officer-citizen interaction (consensual encounter, investigatory stop, arrest)?

- [x] The escalating degree of restraint on the person's freedom to leave and the corresponding evidentiary standard required to justify it (none, reasonable suspicion, probable cause)
- [ ] Whether the interaction takes place indoors or outdoors

- [ ] Whether a supervisor has approved the interaction in advance

**Explanation:** The three classifications form a spectrum: a consensual encounter requires no justification because the person is free to leave; a detention requires reasonable suspicion; an arrest requires probable cause and results in full custody.

## LO 10.4 — Necessary conditions for arrest

### Q25 — Definition
**LO:** 10.4 · **Source:** BPOC 10.4

"Intent," as a necessary condition for arrest, means:

- [x] A peace officer's purpose or intention to take a person into the custody of the law
- [ ] The suspect's intent to commit the underlying offense
- [ ] A written statement of intent filed with the court before the arrest
- [ ] The intent of a witness to testify against the suspect

**Explanation:** One of the four necessary conditions for a lawful arrest is that the officer must actually intend to take the person into custody of the law — not merely detain or question them.

---

### Q26 — Definition
**LO:** 10.4 · **Source:** BPOC 10.4

"Authority," as a necessary condition for arrest, means:

- [x] The arrest must be made under real authority — the officer is legally authorized to make the arrest and it is supported by probable cause
- [ ] The officer must have a supervisor physically present
- [ ] The officer must be off-duty at the time of arrest
- [ ] The arrestee must acknowledge the officer's authority verbally

**Explanation:** "Authority" requires that the officer's arrest power actually exists under the law for the circumstances presented, and that probable cause supports it.

---

### Q27 — Definition
**LO:** 10.4 · **Source:** BPOC 10.4

"Actual seizure," as a necessary condition for arrest, means:

- [x] The person arrested is taken into custody either by physical force or by submission to an assertion of authority
- [ ] The person's property, but not their body, is taken into police custody
- [ ] The person is placed under electronic monitoring
- [ ] The person is merely informed that they are a suspect

**Explanation:** Actual seizure requires either physical restraint or the person's submission to the officer's show of authority — a purely verbal accusation with no restraint and no submission is not yet an arrest.

---

### Q28 — Definition
**LO:** 10.4 · **Source:** BPOC 10.4

"Understanding," as a necessary condition for arrest, means:

- [x] The person to be arrested understands the officer's intention to arrest them
- [ ] The officer understands the elements of the offense charged
- [ ] The arrestee understands and waives their Miranda rights
- [ ] A magistrate understands the grounds for the warrant

**Explanation:** The arrestee must understand that the officer intends to arrest them — this is the fourth of the four necessary conditions for a lawful arrest, alongside Intent, Authority, and Actual seizure.

---

### Q29 — Recall
**LO:** 10.4 · **Source:** BPOC 10.4

Which of the following is NOT one of the four necessary conditions for arrest identified in this chapter?

- [ ] Intent
- [ ] Authority

**Explanation:** The four conditions are Intent, Authority, Actual seizure, and Understanding. "Documentation" (e.g., a written report) is good practice but is not one of the four legal elements that make an arrest lawful.
