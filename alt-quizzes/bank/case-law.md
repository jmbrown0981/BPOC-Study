What is the correct citation for the case holding that police-created exigency does not defeat the exigent-circumstances exception unless the police created the exigency through actual or threatened conduct that violates the Fourth Amendment?

- [x] Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011)
- [ ] Brigham City v. Stuart, 547 U.S. 398 (2006)
- [ ] Illinois v. McArthur, 531 U.S. 326 (2001)
- [ ] Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385 (1978)

**Explanation:** Kentucky v. King, 563 U.S. 452 (2011), is the police-created-exigency case. Brigham City addresses the emergency-aid exception generally, McArthur addresses temporarily securing a home while seeking a warrant, and Mincey addresses the limited scope of a homicide-scene emergency search.

### Q327 — Concept
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** BPOC 10.8

What did the Supreme Court hold in Kentucky v. King?

- [x] The police-created-exigency doctrine does not apply, and the exigent-circumstances exception remains available, so long as the officers did not create the exigency by engaging in or threatening conduct that itself violates the Fourth Amendment; lawful conduct like knocking and announcing does not disqualify an officer from later relying on a resulting exigency
- [ ] Officers may never rely on exigent circumstances if their own lawful investigative conduct contributed in any way to the emergency
- [ ] Knocking and announcing at a door is itself always an unconstitutional act
- [ ] The exigent-circumstances exception was abolished by this decision

**Explanation:** King clarified a previously murky area — officers don't lose the ability to rely on exigent circumstances just because their own lawful actions (like a knock-and-announce) prompted the response inside that created the exigency.

### Q328 — Scenario

Officers investigating a tip lawfully knock on an apartment door and announce their presence. Hearing what sounds like frantic movement and objects being flushed, they force entry without a warrant, believing evidence is being destroyed. - [x] No — King holds that lawful investigative steps like a knock-and-announce do not constitute police-created exigency; the exception remains available because the officers did not create the exigency through conduct that itself violated or threatened to violate the Fourth Amendment
- [ ] Yes — any officer conduct that contributes to a suspect's decision to destroy evidence automatically defeats the exigent-circumstances exception
- [ ] Yes, but only if the officers had probable cause before they knocked
- [ ] No, but only because the officers had already obtained a warrant before knocking

**Explanation:** This scenario is squarely the fact pattern King addressed — lawful, non-coercive investigative conduct like knocking and announcing doesn't disqualify officers from relying on exigent circumstances that result from it.

### Q329 — Concept
**LO:** 10.8 · **Source:** BPOC 10.8

How do Kentucky v. King and Brigham City v. Stuart work together within the broader exigent-circumstances doctrine?

- [x] Brigham City establishes that officers may make a warrantless entry when an objectively reasonable emergency exists, while King clarifies that officers do not forfeit that authority merely because their own lawful conduct (rather than any Fourth Amendment violation) contributed to creating the emergency
- [ ] The two cases directly conflict on whether exigent circumstances can ever justify a warrantless entry
- [ ] King eliminates the objective-reasonableness standard that Brigham City established
- [ ] Brigham City applies only to violence, and has no connection to King's evidence-destruction scenario

**Explanation:** Together, these cases reinforce the same objective, practical approach to exigent circumstances — assessed by what's actually happening and how it arose, not by second-guessing an officer's lawful investigative choices.

### Q330 — Definition

Under Kentucky v. King, the exigent-circumstances exception is defeated only when police create the exigency through conduct that:

- [x] Itself violates or threatens to violate the Fourth Amendment
- [ ] Is loud enough to be heard by neighbors
- [ ] Occurs at night rather than during the day
- [ ] Involves more than one officer approaching the door

**Explanation:** This is King's precise limiting principle — the disqualifying conduct has to be unlawful in itself (or an unlawful threat), not merely conduct that happens to prompt someone inside to react by destroying evidence.
