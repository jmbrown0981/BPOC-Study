# Chapter 8: Penal Code — Definitions Question Bank

*This bank covers only definitions: PC Title 1's general provisions (LO 8.2), the Exception/Defense/Affirmative Defense framework and the components/defenses to criminal responsibility (LO 8.3-8.5), and every offense chapter's own "Definitions" article touched on in BPOC Chapter 8. Substantive offense elements, penalty classifications, and scenario-based application are covered in the companion Chapter 8 offenses/classifications bank.*

## LO 8.2 — General Provisions: Computation of Age & Definitions (PC 1.06, 1.07)

### Q1 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.06

PC 1.06 ("Computation of Age") establishes that a person attains a specified age:

- [x] On the day of the anniversary of their birthdate
- [ ] At 12:01 a.m. the day before their birthdate
- [ ] At the start of the calendar year in which their birthday falls
- [ ] Only once a birth certificate has been produced in court

**Explanation:** "A person attains a specified age on the day of the anniversary of his birthdate." PC 1.06. This is why, for statutes with sharp age-based thresholds (e.g., turning 17), the actual calendar day of the birthday controls.

---

### Q2 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(1)

PC 1.07(a)(1) defines "Act" as:

- [x] A bodily movement, whether voluntary or involuntary, and includes speech
- [ ] Only a voluntary bodily movement
- [ ] Any bodily movement that produces a criminal result
- [ ] A movement made with a culpable mental state

**Explanation:** "'Act' means a bodily movement, whether voluntary or involuntary, and includes speech." Note that the *voluntariness* requirement lives in PC 6.01, not in this definition — an "act" itself can be involuntary; it's the requirement that a person "voluntarily engage" in it that separately matters for criminal liability.

### Q3 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(2)

PC 1.07(a)(2) defines "Actor" as:

- [x] A person whose criminal responsibility is in issue in a criminal action; wherever this code uses "suspect," it means "actor"
- [ ] Only the person who directly caused a victim's injury
- [ ] Any person named in a criminal complaint, including witnesses
- [ ] A person convicted of the offense charged

**Explanation:** "'Actor' means a person whose criminal responsibility is in issue in a criminal action. Whenever the term 'suspect' is used in this code, it means 'actor.'"

---

### Q4 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(8)

PC 1.07(a)(8) defines "Bodily injury" as:

- [x] Physical pain, illness, or any impairment of physical condition
- [ ] Only an injury requiring hospitalization
- [ ] Physical pain lasting longer than 24 hours
- [ ] A wound that leaves a visible mark

**Explanation:** "'Bodily injury' means physical pain, illness, or any impairment of physical condition." This is a deliberately low bar — even brief physical pain qualifies — and it's distinct from the higher "serious bodily injury" standard.

---

### Q5 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(9)

PC 1.07(a)(9) defines "Coercion" as a threat, however communicated, to do which of the following?

- [x] Any of several things, including committing an offense, inflicting future bodily injury, accusing a person of an offense, exposing a person to hatred/contempt/ridicule, harming credit or business reputation, or a public servant taking/withholding official action
- [ ] Only a threat to physically harm the person immediately
- [ ] Only a threat made in writing
- [ ] Only a threat to damage property

**Explanation:** Coercion is defined broadly to include reputational, financial, and official-action threats, not just threats of physical violence — this is what allows "coercion" to do heavy lifting in chapters like Bribery (36.03) and Trafficking (20A.01).

---

### Q6 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(10)

PC 1.07(a)(10) defines "Conduct" as:

- [x] An act or omission and its accompanying mental state
- [ ] Only a voluntary act
- [ ] Any act, regardless of mental state
- [ ] The result caused by an act

**Explanation:** "'Conduct' means an act or omission and its accompanying mental state." This ties together the physical component (act/omission) and the mental component (culpable mental state) into one term used throughout the offense-definition framework.

---

### Q7 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(11)

PC 1.07(a)(11) defines "Consent" as:

- [x] Assent in fact, whether express or apparent
- [ ] Only written, signed assent
- [ ] Assent given without any possibility of later withdrawal
- [ ] Assent that must be witnessed by a third party to be valid

**Explanation:** "'Consent' means assent in fact, whether express or apparent." This general definition is distinct from the more detailed "effective consent" definitions found in specific chapters (like Theft's 31.01), which list circumstances under which consent is *not* effective.

---

### Q8 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(14)

PC 1.07(a)(14) defines "Correctional facility" as a place designated by law for confinement of a person arrested for, charged with, or convicted of a criminal offense, including:

- [x] A municipal or county jail, a TDCJ confinement facility, a facility operated under contract with TDCJ, and a community corrections facility operated by a community supervision and corrections department
- [ ] Only state prisons operated directly by TDCJ
- [ ] Any hospital where an arrestee receives medical treatment
- [ ] Only county jails, excluding municipal jails

**Explanation:** The definition is broader than just "prison" — it explicitly reaches county/municipal jails, contract facilities, and community corrections (halfway house-type) facilities.

---

### Q9 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(17)

PC 1.07(a)(17) defines "Deadly weapon" as:

- [x] A firearm or anything manifestly designed, made, or adapted to inflict death or serious bodily injury, OR anything that in the manner of its use or intended use is capable of causing death or serious bodily injury
- [ ] Only a firearm
- [ ] Only an item specifically manufactured as a weapon
- [ ] Any object that causes any injury, however minor

**Explanation:** This two-pronged definition is why ordinary objects (a vehicle, a belt, boiling water) can legally qualify as "deadly weapons" under the second prong based on how they were used or intended to be used, even though they weren't manifestly designed as weapons.

---

### Q10 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(19)

PC 1.07(a)(19) provides that consent is NOT "effective consent" if it is:

- [x] Induced by force, threat, or fraud; given by someone the actor knows isn't legally authorized to act for the owner; given by someone known to be unable to make reasonable decisions due to youth/mental disease or defect/intoxication; or given solely to detect an offense
- [ ] Given verbally rather than in writing
- [ ] Given by a minor of any age, regardless of the circumstances
- [ ] Withdrawn at any point after being initially given

**Explanation:** This general "effective consent" definition in Chapter 1 mirrors (with slightly different wording) the more detailed version found in Theft's Sec. 31.01(3) — both share the same four disqualifying circumstances.

---

### Q11 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(22)

PC 1.07(a)(22) defines "Element of offense" as consisting of:

- [x] The forbidden conduct, the required culpability, any required result, and the negation of any exception to the offense
- [ ] Only the forbidden conduct
- [ ] The forbidden conduct and the required culpability, but not any result or exception
- [ ] Only the punishment range assigned to the offense

**Explanation:** This four-part definition is the analytical checklist for what the state must prove: what was done (conduct), the mental state behind it (culpability), any consequence required (result), and that no statutory exception applies.

---

### Q12 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(23)

PC 1.07(a)(23) defines "Felony" as:

- [x] An offense so designated by law, or one punishable by death or confinement in a penitentiary
- [ ] Any offense punishable by a fine exceeding $500
- [ ] Any offense that involves violence
- [ ] Any offense punishable by more than 30 days in jail

**Explanation:** "'Felony' means an offense so designated by law or punishable by death or confinement in a penitentiary." Confinement location (penitentiary vs. jail) is a key distinguishing feature from the misdemeanor definition.

---

### Q13 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(26) & (49)

PC 1.07(a)(26) defines "Individual" to include an unborn child at every stage of gestation from fertilization until birth. How does PC 1.07(a)(49) extend this concept?

- [x] "Death" includes, for an individual who is an unborn child, the failure to be born alive
- [ ] "Death" excludes any unborn child regardless of gestational stage
- [ ] "Individual" only includes an unborn child after 20 weeks of gestation
- [ ] "Death" applies to an unborn child only if the child was viable outside the womb

**Explanation:** These two definitions work together and are directly relevant to homicide-adjacent statutes: because "individual" includes an unborn child at every stage of gestation, and "death" for such an individual includes the failure to be born alive, harm resulting in that outcome can implicate criminal homicide provisions (see PC 19.06's "Applicability to Certain Conduct").

---

### Q14 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(30-a)

PC 1.07(a)(30-a) defines "Mass shooting" as a person's discharge of a firearm to cause (or attempt to cause) serious bodily injury or death to how many or more persons?

- [x] Four or more persons, during the same criminal transaction or during different transactions pursuant to the same scheme or course of conduct
- [ ] Two or more persons, during the same criminal transaction only
- [ ] Ten or more persons, during the same criminal transaction only
- [ ] Any number of persons, so long as a firearm is discharged in a public place

**Explanation:** This relatively recent addition to Sec. 1.07 sets the threshold at four or more victims and, notably, doesn't require the victims to be harmed in a single transaction — a scheme or course of conduct across multiple transactions can also qualify.

---

### Q15 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(31)

PC 1.07(a)(31) defines "Misdemeanor" as:

- [x] An offense so designated by law, or one punishable by fine, by confinement in jail, or by both
- [ ] Any offense punishable only by a fine, never by confinement
- [ ] An offense so designated by law, or one punishable by confinement in a penitentiary
- [ ] Any offense less serious than a Class C traffic violation

**Explanation:** Note the contrast with "felony" (PC 1.07(a)(23)): misdemeanors are tied to fines and/or *jail* confinement, while felonies are tied to death or *penitentiary* confinement.

---

### Q16 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(34)

PC 1.07(a)(34) defines "Omission" as:

- [x] Failure to act
- [ ] Any act performed with criminal negligence
- [ ] A statement that is materially false
- [ ] The failure of the state to prove an element of an offense

**Explanation:** "'Omission' means failure to act." Whether an omission can itself be criminal depends on PC 6.01(c) — an omission is only an offense if a law defines it as such or otherwise imposes a duty to act.

---

### Q17 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(35)

PC 1.07(a)(35) defines "Owner" as a person who:

- [x] Has title to the property, possession of the property (whether lawful or not), or a greater right to possession of the property than the actor — or who is a holder in due course of a negotiable instrument
- [ ] Only a person who holds a formal, recorded legal title to the property
- [ ] Only a person with lawful possession of the property
- [ ] Only the original manufacturer or creator of the property

**Explanation:** Notably, even someone in *unlawful* possession can be an "owner" for purposes of this definition, as long as they have a greater right to possession than the actor — this matters, for example, when a thief steals from another thief.

---

### Q18 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(36)

PC 1.07(a)(36) defines "Peace officer" by reference to:

- [x] A person elected, employed, or appointed as a peace officer under CCP Art. 2A.001, Education Code Sec. 51.212 or 51.214, or other law
- [ ] Only a person who has completed TCOLE basic peace officer licensing
- [ ] Any person carrying a firearm while on duty for a governmental agency
- [ ] Only commissioned municipal police officers

**Explanation:** The Penal Code definition cross-references the Code of Criminal Procedure's peace officer list (Art. 2A.001) rather than independently defining the term.

---

### Q19 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(38)

PC 1.07(a)(38) defines "Person" as:

- [x] An individual or a corporation, association, limited liability company, or other entity or organization governed by the Business Organizations Code
- [ ] Only a human being
- [ ] Only an individual who has reached the age of majority
- [ ] Any entity capable of owning real property

**Explanation:** "Person" is broader than "individual" (PC 1.07(a)(26)) — it includes business entities, which matters for offenses that can be committed by or against a "person" in a corporate sense.

---

### Q20 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(39)

PC 1.07(a)(39) defines "Possession" as:

- [x] Actual care, custody, control, or management
- [ ] Physical touching of an item at any point in time
- [ ] Legal ownership of an item, regardless of its current location
- [ ] Storage of an item on premises the person owns

**Explanation:** "'Possession' means actual care, custody, control, or management." Combined with PC 6.01(b), possession only becomes a voluntary act (and thus potentially criminal) if the possessor knowingly obtained/received the thing or was aware of controlling it for long enough to terminate that control.

---

### Q21 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(40)

PC 1.07(a)(40) defines "Public place" as any place to which the public or a substantial group of the public has access, including but not limited to:

- [x] Streets, highways, and the common areas of schools, hospitals, apartment houses, office buildings, transport facilities, and shops
- [ ] Only government-owned property
- [ ] Only outdoor locations
- [ ] Only locations where no admission fee is charged

**Explanation:** The list is illustrative, not exhaustive ("includes, but is not limited to") — the operative test is public/substantial-group access, not who owns the property or whether it's indoors or outdoors.

### Q22 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(41)

PC 1.07(a)(41) defines "Public servant" to include which of the following categories?

- [x] An officer/employee/agent of government, a juror or grand juror, an arbitrator/referee authorized to hear a controversy, an attorney or notary performing a governmental function, a candidate for public office, and a person performing a governmental function under a claim of right even if not legally qualified
- [ ] Only elected officials
- [ ] Only persons who have taken a formal oath of office
- [ ] Only full-time government employees, excluding volunteers and candidates

**Explanation:** This definition is deliberately expansive — it reaches jurors, candidates for office, and even someone falsely claiming governmental authority, which matters for offenses like Impersonating a Public Servant (37.11) and Bribery (36.01-36.02).

### Q23 — Definition
**LO:** 8.2 · **Source:** PC 1.07(a)(42)

PC 1.07(a)(42) defines "Reasonable belief" as a belief that would be held by:

- [x] An ordinary and prudent man in the same circumstances as the actor
- [ ] A trained peace officer with at least five years of experience
- [ ] A licensed attorney evaluating the same facts
- [ ] Any person, regardless of the circumstances they were in

**Explanation:** "'Reasonable belief' means a belief that would be held by an ordinary and prudent man in the same circumstances as the actor." This objective, circumstance-specific standard appears throughout the Penal Code wherever an actor's justification for their conduct is at issue.

### Q25 — Definition

## LO 8.3 — Exception, Defense, and Affirmative Defense (PC 2.02–2.04)

### Q30 — Definition
**LO:** 8.3 · **Source:** PC 2.02

An "Exception" under PC 2.02 is labeled in a statute by which phrase?

- [x] "It is an exception to the application of . . . ."
- [ ] "It is a defense to prosecution . . . ."
- [ ] "It is an affirmative defense to prosecution . . . ."
- [ ] "The state shall not prosecute . . . ."

**Explanation:** PC 2.02(a): "An exception to an offense in this code is so labeled by the phrase: 'It is an exception to the application of . . . .'"

---

### Q31 — Concept
**LO:** 8.3 · **Source:** PC 2.02(b)

For an "Exception," who bears the burden of proof, and what is that burden?

- [x] The prosecuting attorney must negate the existence of the exception in the charging instrument and prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's conduct does not fall within the exception
- [ ] The defendant must prove the exception applies by a preponderance of the evidence
- [ ] Neither party bears any burden — the judge decides as a matter of law
- [ ] The defendant must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the exception does not apply

**Explanation:** Exceptions place the heaviest burden on the state among the three categories — the state must affirmatively negate the exception and disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt, unlike defenses and affirmative defenses.

---

### Q32 — Definition
**LO:** 8.3 · **Source:** PC 2.03

A "Defense" under PC 2.03 is labeled by which phrase, and what happens procedurally?

- [x] Labeled "It is a defense to prosecution . . . ."; the state need not negate it in the charging instrument, it only goes to the jury if evidence supports it, and a reasonable doubt on the issue requires acquittal
- [ ] Labeled "It is an affirmative defense to prosecution . . . ."; the defendant must prove it by a preponderance of the evidence
- [ ] Labeled "It is an exception to the application of . . . ."; the state must negate it in the charging instrument
- [ ] Labeled the same way as an exception, with identical procedural consequences

**Explanation:** PC 2.03: the state isn't required to negate a defense in the charging instrument, and the defense only reaches the jury if evidence supports it — but once it does, a reasonable doubt on that issue means acquittal, same practical effect as with an exception.

---

### Q33 — Definition
**LO:** 8.3 · **Source:** PC 2.04

An "Affirmative Defense" under PC 2.04 is labeled by which phrase, and who bears the burden of proof once it's raised?

- [x] Labeled "It is an affirmative defense to prosecution . . . ."; the defendant must prove it by a preponderance of the evidence
- [ ] Labeled "It is a defense to prosecution . . . ."; the state must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt
- [ ] Labeled the same as an exception; the state bears the burden
- [ ] No specific label is required; the burden always falls on the state

**Explanation:** PC 2.04 is the only one of the three categories where the burden of proof shifts to the defendant — and even then, only to the lower "preponderance of the evidence" standard, not beyond a reasonable doubt.

---

### Q34 — Concept
**LO:** 8.3 · **Source:** PC 2.02–2.04

Ranking Exception, Defense, and Affirmative Defense from most favorable to the STATE'S burden of proof to least, which order is correct?

- [x] Exception (state must disprove beyond reasonable doubt) is most burdensome on the state; Defense (state need not negate, but reasonable doubt still acquits) is a middle ground; Affirmative Defense (defendant proves by preponderance) is least burdensome on the state
- [ ] Affirmative Defense is most burdensome on the state; Exception is least burdensome
- [ ] All three impose an identical burden on the state
- [ ] Defense is most burdensome; Exception and Affirmative Defense are identical

**Explanation:** The three categories exist precisely to allocate burdens differently — Exception requires the state to proactively disprove it, Defense requires the state to overcome it if raised (jury acquits on reasonable doubt), and Affirmative Defense shifts the actual burden of persuasion to the defendant.

## LO 8.4 — Components of a Penal Code Offense (PC 6.01–6.04)

### Q35 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.01(a)

PC 6.01(a) provides that a person commits an offense only if they:

- [x] Voluntarily engage in conduct, including an act, an omission, or possession
- [ ] Intend to cause a specific result, regardless of voluntariness
- [ ] Engage in any conduct, whether voluntary or involuntary
- [ ] Are physically present at the scene of an offense

**Explanation:** "A person commits an offense only if he voluntarily engages in conduct, including an act, an omission, or possession." Voluntariness is the threshold requirement before culpability (PC 6.02) is even considered.

---

### Q36 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.01(b)

Per PC 6.01(b), when is possession a "voluntary act"?

- [x] When the possessor knowingly obtains or receives the thing possessed, or is aware of their control of it for a sufficient time to permit them to terminate that control
- [ ] Whenever an item is physically located on the possessor's property
- [ ] Only if the possessor purchased the item themselves
- [ ] Possession can never be a voluntary act under Texas law

**Explanation:** This is the classic "planted evidence" or "unknowing carrier" protection — if someone doesn't know they have an item and hasn't had a reasonable opportunity to discover and get rid of it, their possession isn't a voluntary act.

---

### Q37 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.01(c)

Per PC 6.01(c), when does an omission (failure to act) become a criminal offense?

- [x] Only if a law provides that the omission is an offense, or otherwise provides that the person has a duty to perform the act
- [ ] Whenever a person fails to help someone in an emergency
- [ ] Whenever a person's inaction results in harm to another
- [ ] An omission can never form the basis of a criminal offense

**Explanation:** Texas doesn't impose a general legal duty to act — an omission is only criminal if some specific law creates that duty (e.g., a parent's duty to a child, or specific statutory reporting requirements).

---

### Q38 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.02(a)-(c)

Per PC 6.02, if a statute defining an offense does not prescribe a culpable mental state, is one still required?

- [x] Yes — a culpable mental state is nevertheless required unless the statute plainly dispenses with any mental element; and if one is required but not specified, intent, knowledge, or recklessness suffices
- [ ] No — silence on mental state always means no mental state is required
- [ ] Yes, but only criminal negligence ever fills the gap
- [ ] The offense is void for vagueness if it doesn't specify a mental state

**Explanation:** This default rule prevents strict-liability offenses from being created by accident — the legislature must plainly dispense with a mental element for an offense to require none.

---

### Q39 — Recall
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.02(d)

PC 6.02(d) ranks the four culpable mental states from highest to lowest degree. What is that order?

- [x] Intentional, knowing, reckless, criminal negligence
- [ ] Knowing, intentional, criminal negligence, reckless
- [ ] Reckless, knowing, intentional, criminal negligence
- [ ] Criminal negligence, reckless, knowing, intentional

**Explanation:** This ranking matters because PC 6.02(e) provides that proof of a higher degree of culpability than charged constitutes proof of the culpability actually charged — e.g., proving intentional conduct also proves knowing, reckless, and criminally negligent conduct.

---

### Q40 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.03(a)

PC 6.03(a) defines acting "intentionally" as:

- [x] When it is the actor's conscious objective or desire to engage in the conduct or cause the result
- [ ] When the actor is merely aware that the conduct or result is reasonably certain to occur
- [ ] When the actor consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk
- [ ] When the actor should have been aware of a risk but wasn't

**Explanation:** Intent is the highest culpable mental state — it requires the conduct or result to be the actor's actual conscious objective, not merely something they were aware of or should have anticipated.

---

### Q41 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.03(b)

PC 6.03(b) defines acting "knowingly" with respect to a *result* of conduct as:

- [x] Being aware that the conduct is reasonably certain to cause the result
- [ ] Having a conscious objective or desire to cause the result
- [ ] Consciously disregarding a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur
- [ ] Failing to perceive a risk that an ordinary person would have perceived

**Explanation:** Note the distinction from "intentionally": knowledge as to a result requires awareness that it's reasonably certain to happen, not that causing it was the actor's actual goal.

---

### Q42 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.03(c)

PC 6.03(c) defines acting "recklessly" as:

- [x] Being aware of, but consciously disregarding, a substantial and unjustifiable risk — where the disregard is a gross deviation from the standard of care an ordinary person would exercise
- [ ] Failing to be aware of a risk that an ordinary person would have perceived
- [ ] Having a conscious objective to cause a specific result
- [ ] Any conduct that happens to cause an unintended result

**Explanation:** The key distinguishing feature of recklessness (versus criminal negligence) is *awareness* — a reckless actor is aware of the risk and disregards it anyway; a criminally negligent actor isn't even aware of a risk they should have perceived.

---

### Q43 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.03(d)

PC 6.03(d) defines acting with "criminal negligence" as:

- [x] Ought to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk — where the failure to perceive it is a gross deviation from the standard of care an ordinary person would exercise
- [ ] Being aware of a risk and consciously disregarding it
- [ ] Having a conscious objective or desire to cause the result
- [ ] Being aware that a result is reasonably certain to occur

**Explanation:** Criminal negligence is the lowest of the four culpable mental states — unlike recklessness, it doesn't require actual awareness of the risk, only that a reasonable person in the actor's position ought to have been aware of it.

---

### Q44 — Definition
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.04(a)

PC 6.04(a) ("Causation") provides that a person is criminally responsible for a result if:

- [x] The result would not have occurred but for their conduct, operating alone or concurrently with another cause — unless the concurrent cause was clearly sufficient to produce the result and the actor's conduct was clearly insufficient
- [ ] Their conduct was the sole and only cause of the result, with no other contributing factors
- [ ] Their conduct merely increased the likelihood of the result occurring
- [ ] The result was reasonably foreseeable at the time of the conduct, regardless of but-for causation

**Explanation:** This is Texas's "but-for" causation test with a carve-out: if another cause alone would have clearly been enough to produce the result, and the defendant's own conduct was clearly not enough on its own, causation is not established.

---

### Q45 — Concept
**LO:** 8.4 · **Source:** PC 6.04(b)

Per PC 6.04(b), a person remains criminally responsible for causing a result even if the actual outcome differs from what they desired, contemplated, or risked, so long as the only difference is that:

- [x] A different offense was committed, or a different person or property was injured, harmed, or otherwise affected
- [ ] The result occurred at a different time than planned
- [ ] The actor changed their mind partway through the conduct
- [ ] A codefendant, rather than the actor, directly caused the result

**Explanation:** This is the "transferred intent" style principle — for example, if a person shoots at one target intending to kill them but instead kills a bystander, the difference in *who* was harmed doesn't defeat criminal responsibility.

## LO 8.5 — General Defenses to Criminal Responsibility (PC 8.01–8.07)

### Q46 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.01

PC 8.01 ("Insanity") makes it an affirmative defense to prosecution that, at the time of the conduct charged, the actor:

- [x] As a result of severe mental disease or defect, did not know that their conduct was wrong
- [ ] Was under the influence of any mental illness, however mild
- [ ] Did not intend to commit the specific offense charged
- [ ] Was legally incompetent to stand trial

**Explanation:** "It is an affirmative defense to prosecution that, at the time of the conduct charged, the actor, as a result of severe mental disease or defect, did not know that his conduct was wrong." Note this is an affirmative defense (defendant proves by preponderance), and it explicitly excludes "an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct."

---

### Q47 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.02

PC 8.02 ("Mistake of Fact") is a defense to prosecution when:

- [x] The actor, through mistake, formed a reasonable belief about a matter of fact, and that mistaken belief negated the kind of culpability required for the offense
- [ ] The actor was simply unaware that their conduct was illegal
- [ ] The actor relied on incorrect legal advice from an attorney
- [ ] The actor's mistake was unreasonable but sincerely held

**Explanation:** Mistake of fact addresses factual misunderstandings (e.g., reasonably believing property was one's own), not misunderstandings about what the law itself requires — that's mistake of *law* (PC 8.03), and it's treated very differently.

---

### Q48 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.03

PC 8.03 ("Mistake of Law") establishes that ignorance of the law is generally no defense, but recognizes a narrow affirmative defense when the actor reasonably relied on:

- [x] An official written statement of the law by a responsible administrative agency, or a written interpretation of the law in a court opinion or by a public official charged with interpreting it
- [ ] Advice from a friend or family member who claimed to be knowledgeable about the law
- [ ] Their own good-faith belief that the conduct was probably legal
- [ ] Any internet source describing the law, regardless of authorship

**Explanation:** "It is no defense to prosecution that the actor was ignorant of the provisions of any law after the law has taken effect" — but a narrow affirmative defense exists for reasonable reliance on *official* written legal guidance, not informal or self-generated beliefs.

---

### Q49 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.04

PC 8.04 ("Intoxication") establishes what rule regarding voluntary intoxication?

- [x] Voluntary intoxication does not constitute a defense to the commission of a crime, though evidence of temporary insanity caused by intoxication may be introduced in mitigation of punishment
- [ ] Voluntary intoxication is always a complete defense to any offense
- [ ] Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to offenses requiring specific intent
- [ ] Involuntary and voluntary intoxication are treated identically under this section

**Explanation:** "Voluntary intoxication does not constitute a defense to the commission of crime" — but temporary-insanity evidence tied to intoxication can still be used at the punishment stage, not to avoid conviction but to potentially reduce the sentence.

---

### Q50 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.05

PC 8.05 ("Duress") is an affirmative defense when the actor engaged in the proscribed conduct because they were compelled to do so by:

- [x] Threat of imminent death or serious bodily injury to themselves or another (for felonies); or, for non-felony offenses, force or threat of force
- [ ] Any verbal pressure from another person, regardless of severity
- [ ] A court order compelling the conduct
- [ ] Financial hardship or economic necessity alone

**Explanation:** Duress requires genuine compulsion rising to the level of threatened imminent death/serious bodily injury for felonies (a lower threshold of force/threat of force applies for non-felonies) — and the defense is unavailable if the actor intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly placed themselves in a situation where compulsion was probable.

---

### Q51 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.06

PC 8.06 ("Entrapment") is a defense to prosecution when the actor engaged in the charged conduct because they were:

- [x] Induced to do so by a law enforcement agent using persuasion or other means likely to cause persons to commit the offense; merely affording an opportunity to commit an offense does NOT constitute entrapment
- [ ] Simply approached by an undercover officer who asked if they wanted to buy contraband
- [ ] Present at a location where a law enforcement sting operation was occurring
- [ ] Told by a law enforcement agent that the conduct was legal

**Explanation:** The key distinction is between inducement (persuasion/pressure likely to cause an otherwise-unwilling person to commit an offense — entrapment) and merely providing an opportunity to commit a crime the person was already willing to commit (not entrapment).

---

### Q52 — Definition
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.07(a)

PC 8.07(a) generally bars prosecution for an offense committed when the actor was younger than what age, subject to a list of specific exceptions (like perjury, fine-only misdemeanors, and certain transferred/certified felony cases)?

- [x] 15 years of age
- [ ] 10 years of age
- [ ] 17 years of age
- [ ] 18 years of age

**Explanation:** PC 8.07(a)'s general floor is age 15, with several enumerated exceptions (e.g., perjury/aggravated perjury if the person had sufficient discretion to understand an oath, certain traffic and fine-only offenses, and certain serious felonies where a juvenile has been certified for adult prosecution).

---

### Q53 — Concept
**LO:** 8.5 · **Source:** PC 8.07(b)-(e)

Which of the following correctly summarizes an additional age-related rule found in PC 8.07(b)-(e)?

- [x] Absent a juvenile court waiver/certification, a person generally may not be prosecuted for an offense committed before age 17 (except certain listed offenses), and a person age 10-14 is presumed incapable of committing a fine-only/penal-ordinance offense unless the state rebuts that presumption
- [ ] A person may never be prosecuted as an adult for any offense committed before age 18, without exception
- [ ] The death penalty may be imposed for an offense committed at age 17 but not age 16
- [ ] There is no minimum age below which prosecution is completely barred

**Explanation:** PC 8.07 layers several age thresholds: a general 15-year floor with exceptions (subsection (a)), a general 17-year floor absent juvenile certification (subsection (b)), no death penalty under age 18 (subsection (c)), an absolute bar under age 10 for fine-only/penal-ordinance offenses (subsection (d)), and a rebuttable presumption of incapacity for ages 10-14 as to those same offenses (subsection (e)).

## LO 8.35 — Organized Crime Definitions (PC 71.01)

### Q111 — Definition
**LO:** 8.35 · **Source:** PC 71.01(a)

PC 71.01(a) defines "Combination" as three or more persons who collaborate in carrying on criminal activities, even though:

- [x] Participants may not know each other's identity, membership may change over time, and participants may stand in a wholesaler-retailer or other arm's-length relationship in illicit distribution operations
- [ ] All members must know each other personally and meet in person regularly
- [ ] Membership must remain fixed and unchanging for the combination to exist
- [ ] Participants must have a formal written agreement establishing the combination

- [ ] Proof that the underlying offense was actually completed
- [ ] At least one conspirator must have a prior felony conviction

**Explanation:** The overt-act requirement distinguishes a punishable conspiracy from mere talk or planning — but that overt act can be proven circumstantially, through the parties' conduct, rather than requiring direct evidence of an explicit plan.

**Explanation:** All three concepts (Combination, Criminal street gang, Foreign terrorist organization) share the three-or-more-persons threshold but are defined around different organizing features — general criminal collaboration, an identifying symbol/leadership structure, or a partly foreign-based threat to state security — and any one of the three can support an Engaging in Organized Criminal Activity charge (71.02).
