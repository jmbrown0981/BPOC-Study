### Q4 — Concept

**Explanation:** Anderson v. Creighton and Harlow v. Fitzgerald are foundational qualified immunity cases, shielding officers from personal liability unless they violate clearly established rights.

### Q5 — Concept
**LO:** 28.1 · **Source:** BPOC 28.1

Graham v. Connor, listed as a case study under this LO, established what framework for evaluating an officer's use-of-force decision?

- [ ] A strict liability standard for any injury caused during an arrest
- [ ] A standard requiring proof of malicious intent

**Explanation:** Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989), established the objective reasonableness standard, judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on scene without the benefit of hindsight.

### Q6 — Fill-in-Blank

PC 9.05 addresses ______, providing that an actor who recklessly injures or kills an innocent third person loses the Chapter 9 justification in a prosecution for that injury or death.

- [ ] Threats as Justifiable Force

**Explanation:** PC 9.05, Reckless Injury of Innocent Third Person: even though force against the attacker is justified, "the justification afforded by this chapter is unavailable in a prosecution for the reckless injury or killing of the innocent third person."

### Q7 — Concept
**LO:** 28.1 · **Source:** Penal Code 9.04

Under Penal Code 9.04, an officer points a pistol at a suspect only to make the suspect believe deadly force will be used if necessary. How is that treated?

- [x] It is a threat of force, not the use of deadly force, as long as the purpose is limited to creating that apprehension
- [ ] It is the use of deadly force and must meet Penal Code 9.32 or 9.51(c)

- [ ] It is an assault by threat in every case

**Explanation:** Penal Code 9.04: the threat of force is justified when the use of force is justified, and producing a weapon to create an apprehension that deadly force will be used if necessary "does not constitute the use of deadly force."

### Q12 — Concept

### Q15 — Recall
**LO:** 28.1 · **Source:** Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989)

Which three factors did Graham v. Connor identify for judging whether force was objectively reasonable?

- [ ] The officer's intent, the officer's experience, and the suspect's criminal history
- [ ] Whether a warrant existed, the time of day, and the number of witnesses
- [ ] The suspect's age, the officer's training, and the department's policy
- [x] The severity of the crime, whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of officers or others, and whether the suspect is actively resisting or attempting to evade arrest by flight

**Explanation:** Graham v. Connor: reasonableness is judged "from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, rather than with the 20/20 vision of hindsight," allowing for split-second judgments, and the officer's underlying intent or motivation does not matter.

### Q16 — Concept
**LO:** 28.1 · **Source:** Graham v. Connor (1989)

Under Graham v. Connor, does an officer's bad intent make otherwise objectively reasonable force unconstitutional?

- [x] No — the test is objective; an officer's evil intentions won't make reasonable force unconstitutional, and good intentions won't make unreasonable force constitutional
- [ ] Only if the officer admits it
- [ ] Only in Texas state court

**Explanation:** Graham v. Connor applies the Fourth Amendment's objective reasonableness standard to all claims of excessive force during an arrest, investigatory stop, or other seizure.

### Q17 — Recall
**LO:** 28.1 · **Source:** Brower v. County of Inyo (1989)

In Brower v. County of Inyo, what did the Supreme Court decide about a roadblock that a fleeing driver crashed into and died?
